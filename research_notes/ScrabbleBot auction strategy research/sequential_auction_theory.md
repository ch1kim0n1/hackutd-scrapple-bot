# Optimal Bidding in Repeated, Budget-Constrained Sequential Second-Price Auctions of Complementary Items (incl. Trading Agent Competitions)

Game being mapped: 6 players, 100 starting currency, 5 private tiles. About 68 letter tiles are sold one at a time in sealed-bid second-price (Vickrey) auctions, one per second. Words pay reward that counts both as score and as new spendable currency, with length multipliers (<=3: x1, 4: x1.5, 5: x2, 6: x2.5, 7+: x3). Tiles are strong complements, so the bot faces an exposure problem. Highest final score wins, and leftover money is worthless at game end.

Labels used below: **[THEORY]** means a proven result in a stylized model. **[EMPIRICAL]** means competition, simulation, lab or field evidence. **[INFERENCE]** means my own mapping to this game; it is not a sourced claim.

One modeling point drives most of the advice that follows. In this game, score is the objective and money is only an input. Paying a price does not reduce score. Money matters only through its *reinvestment value* before the game ends, and that value falls to zero at the end. In the autobidding literature's terms, the bot is therefore closer to a **value maximizer with a budget** than to a quasi-linear **utility maximizer**. The two lead to different bid formulas (see Q1).

## Q1. What is the optimal or near-optimal bid in sequential second-price auctions with budget constraints? When does truthful bidding fail, and how should a bidder shade for the continuation value of money?

### Takeaway
Truthful bidding stays optimal *within a single auction* only once "value" is redefined as the continuation-value difference. The exact DP bid is the largest price at which winning-and-paying leaves expected final outcome no worse than losing: b* = max{δ ≤ balance : W(m−δ, H∪{x}, t+1) ≥ W(m, H, t+1)}. Two closed-form approximations follow. For a quasi-linear bidder, bid v/(1+V′(m)). For a value maximizer, bid v/α, where α is the shadow price of money in score units. Here α should fall toward 0 as the game ends, so bids should rise and can exceed the tile's immediate score value.

### Cited Findings
- **[THEORY]** Gummadi, Key & Proutiere model repeated second-price auctions under a budget as a discounted MDP whose state is (balance b, current valuation v). They allow income a ≥ 0 per period, with budget dynamics b(i+1) = b(i) + a − b0(i)·1{win}. Without a budget, the auctions decouple and truthful bidding is dominant. With a budget, truthful bidding "may not be sustainable" because the bidder can run out of money. — [Gummadi, Key, Proutiere, "Repeated Auctions under Budget Constraints: Optimal bidding strategies and Equilibria" (SSRN 2066175 / MSR)](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2016/02/SSRN-id2066175.pdf)
- **[THEORY]** In that model the optimal bid is u*(v,b) = min(λ⁻¹(v,b), b), where λ(b0,b) = b0 + e^{−β}(V(b+a) − V(b+a−b0)). Read λ as the opponent's bid "magnified" by the opportunity cost of spending b0 now rather than in future auctions. With continuous bids this reduces to **u* = v / (1 + V′(B))**, so **1/(1+V′(B)) is the bid-shading factor**. — [Gummadi et al.](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2016/02/SSRN-id2066175.pdf)
- **[THEORY]** Two forces set the amount of shading. *Option value*: losing now keeps the chance to win an equivalent item later at a lower price, which pushes toward shading. *Limited patience*: discounting moderates the shading. The shading "increases with time, as the balance drops", and it persists rather than vanishing as it does in learning-driven overbidding. — [Gummadi et al.](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2016/02/SSRN-id2066175.pdf)
- **[THEORY]** Cai et al. use the same MDP for budgeted bidding where the reward is *clicks, not money*. The state is (t remaining auctions, b remaining budget, x features). They define g(δ) = θ(x) + V(t−1, b−δ) − V(t−1, b), where θ is the value of winning. Since g is monotone decreasing in δ, the optimal bid is: if g(b) ≥ 0, bid the whole balance b; otherwise bid the largest integer A with g(A) ≥ 0 and g(A+1) < 0. In other words, bid the price at which immediate value equals the value-function loss from spending. — [Cai et al., "Real-Time Bidding by Reinforcement Learning in Display Advertising", WSDM 2017 (arXiv 1701.02490)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1701.02490)
- **[EMPIRICAL]** Cai et al. approximated V with neural nets for scale. They report 16.7% and 7.4% gains over state-of-the-art baselines on two large datasets, and a 44.7% click improvement in an online A/B test against "a most widely used method in the industry". They argue that budgeted repeated-auction bids "may not be truthful". — [Cai et al. 2017](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1701.02490)
- **[THEORY]** For a budget-constrained *utility maximizer* in a truthful auction, the Lagrangian bid is **b_j = v_j/(1+α_B)**, where α_B is the budget dual (introduced by Balseiro et al. 2015). The general formula for truthful auctions is b_j = v_j·(…)/(λ + Σ_c α_c), with λ = 1 for a utility maximizer and λ = 0 for a value maximizer. Given optimal duals, this "results in an auction outcome identical to an optimal primal solution" (Theorem 3.1 of the survey). — [Aggarwal et al., "Auto-bidding and Auctions in Online Advertising: A Survey", SIGecom Exchanges 22(1), 2024](https://sigecom.org/exchanges/volume_22/1/AGGARWAL.pdf)
- **[THEORY]** The survey distinguishes two objectives. *Value maximization* maximizes Σ x_j v_j "regardless of cost", and payment enters only through constraints. *Utility maximization* maximizes Σ (x_j v_j − p_j). The survey also defines a hybrid λ ∈ [0,1] objective Σ (x_j v_j − λ p_j). — [Aggarwal et al. 2024](https://sigecom.org/exchanges/volume_22/1/AGGARWAL.pdf)
- **[THEORY]** In a two-unit sequential second-price auction with private budgets (3+ bidders), the unique symmetric equilibrium has bidders bid **min(value, budget) in the last auction**. First-round aggressiveness rises with competition (the chance of facing high-budget rivals) and falls with the option value of the second auction. — [Ghosh & Liu, "Sequential second-price auctions with private budgets", GEB 113 (2019) 611–632](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:gamebe:v:113:y:2019:i:c:p:611-632)
- **[THEORY]** With budget constraints and multiple objects, it can pay to bid aggressively on one object to "raise a rival's costs" and deplete the rival's budget, so the next object comes cheaper. A bidder's payoff then depends on the price a rival pays, and this effect exists only when several objects are sold. — [Benoît & Krishna, "Multiple-Object Auctions with Budget Constrained Bidders" (1999 WP; RES 68(1) 2001, 155–179)](https://econwpa.ub.uni-muenchen.de/econ-wp/game/papers/9805/9805001.pdf)
- **[THEORY]** When agents maximize a convex combination of their own profit and their competitors' losses (spite), symmetric Bayes-Nash equilibria change. In second-price auctions the spiteful equilibrium strategy does not depend on the number of bidders. Second-price revenue exceeds first-price revenue for even mildly spiteful agents. — [Brandt, Sandholm & Shoham, "Spiteful Bidding in Sealed-Bid Auctions", IJCAI 2007 / Dagstuhl](https://drops.dagstuhl.de/opus/volltexte/2005/198)

### Inferences
- **[INFERENCE] Core bid rule for this game.** Define W(m, H, t) as the expected final score (or win probability) given money m, holdings H (rack plus private tiles) and auction index t. The exact Vickrey-consistent bid for tile x is the indifference price
  `b*(x) = max{ δ ∈ [0, m] : W(m − δ, H ∪ {x}, t+1) ≥ W(m, H, t+1) }`.
  This is the Cai et al. rule with "clicks" replaced by "score". Bidding b* is optimal in the current auction given W, because second-price makes the price paid independent of your bid. W already contains the reinvestment value of money, so no separate shading term is needed.
- **[INFERENCE] Linearized form (cheap).** With ΔS(x) = E[score | win x] − E[score | lose] at fixed money, and α_t = ∂W/∂m (score per currency unit), the rule is **b ≈ min(m, ΔS(x)/α_t)**. Early on, money compounds: a currency unit buys tiles that pay back as both score and new currency, so α_t can be well above 1 score/coin and bids sit *below* score value. Late in the game α_t → 0, so bids → m, meaning all-in for any tile with positive expected score gain. This is the value-maximizer regime of the autobidding survey (λ = 0), where the multiplier 1/α can exceed 1.
- **[INFERENCE] Do not copy the ad-auction formula v/(1+α) literally.** That formula assumes money spent is utility lost. Here money spent is not score lost. Leftover money is worth zero, so the quasi-linear "1 +" term in the denominator is wrong at game end and overstates shading throughout.
- **[INFERENCE] Endgame consequence.** Ghosh–Liu's last-auction rule bid = min(value, budget) plus zero terminal value of money means that in the final few auctions every rational bot bids its entire balance for any useful tile. The tile then goes to the *highest cash balance*, at a price equal to the second-highest balance. Cash rank is therefore a strategic asset for the last auctions. Plan the spend so the bot is not cash-dominated by rivals when high-value finishing tiles appear.
- **[INFERENCE] Relative objective.** "Highest final score wins" makes rivals' outcomes matter, as in Benoît–Krishna and Brandt et al. When the bot loses a tile, setting a high second price drains the winner's money. That cuts the winner's future buying power, which is worth α_rival × price in the rival's score units. A modest *upward* adjustment over b* on tiles the bot is unlikely to win is supported directionally. The risk is winning a tile at that inflated price.

### Gaps
- I did not retrieve the explicit spiteful second-price bid function, so I cannot give a numeric overbid formula. Morgan, Steiglitz & Reis (2003) and Brandt et al. (full text) would have it.
- No source studies a game where winnings (rewards) replenish the budget with score-equivalent currency and money has zero terminal value. The mapping above is my inference. Gummadi et al.'s constant income rate a is the closest analogue, but it is exogenous.
- The full text of Balseiro, Besbes & Weintraub 2015 (fluid mean-field, v/(1+μ)) was not retrieved; it is cited only via the survey.

## Q2. What do the declining price anomaly, demand reduction, and budget-constrained sequential auction papers predict about prices over time and about buying early vs late?

### Takeaway
Theory gives no universal price trend. With unit demand and risk neutrality, expected prices are a martingale (flat). Risk aversion, positive synergies (complements) and asymmetric multi-unit demand predict *declining* prices. Symmetric multi-unit demand predicts *rising* prices, and budget constraints make order and price paths depend on who holds the money. Two features of this game push prices *up* late: currency injected by word rewards, and money being worthless at the end. Committing early is valuable for flexibility on complements, but it carries exposure.

### Cited Findings
- **[THEORY]** Weber (1983): in sequential auctions of identical objects with risk-neutral unit-demand bidders and independent private values, expected prices are the same in every round (a martingale), and first- and second-price formats are revenue-equivalent. — cited in [Mezzetti, "Sequential auctions with informational externalities and aversion to price risk" (UCLouvain DP)](https://sites.uclouvain.be/econ/DP/REL/2004043.pdf) and [Lebrun 2024](https://blebrun.info.yorku.ca/files/2024/09/priceexpectIPVSSPAlebrunseptember2024-Copy.pdf)
- **[EMPIRICAL]** Declining prices for identical lots in sequential auctions are documented for wine (Ashenfelter 1989; McAfee & Vincent 1993), flowers (van den Berg et al. 2001) and livestock (Buccola 1982). — [Mezzetti DP](https://sites.uclouvain.be/econ/DP/REL/2004043.pdf); [Salladarré et al. (INRAE)](https://hal.inrae.fr/hal-02796465v1/file/publis16-mistea-028_salladarr%C3%A9_declining_1.pdf)
- **[THEORY]** McAfee & Vincent (JET 60, 1993): risk-averse bidders bid in early rounds the expected later price *plus a risk premium*, which yields declining prices. The explanation requires *nondecreasing absolute risk aversion* for pure-strategy equilibria to exist, an assumption the authors find implausible. — [McAfee & Vincent, "The Declining Price Anomaly"](https://econ.umd.edu/publication/declining-price-anomaly)
- **[THEORY]** Mezzetti proposes aversion to *price* risk (not wealth risk) as the driver. The result holds for more than two rounds, for second-price, first-price and English formats, and with informational externalities. — [Mezzetti DP](https://sites.uclouvain.be/econ/DP/REL/2004043.pdf)
- **[THEORY]** **Menezes & Monteiro (Rev. Econ. Design 8, 2003): in sequential second-price auctions, positive synergies (super-additive bundle values) imply declining expected prices, and negative synergies imply increasing ones.** Their 2004 Econ. Letters note shows that with one multi-demand bidder against single-unit rivals, expected prices can *increase*, because single-unit bidders shade in round 1. — [Menezes & Monteiro, "Synergies and price trends in sequential auctions"](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:spr:reecde:v:8:y:2003:i:1:p:85-98)
- **[THEORY]** Katzman (JET 1999): in two sequential second-price auctions with diminishing marginal values, symmetric incomplete information gives efficient outcomes and *increasing* expected prices. With sufficiently heterogeneous (asymmetric) bidders, expected prices can *decrease*. Rodriguez (2009) constructs complete-information equilibria with declining prices. — [Katzman abstract](https://academicnewsletter.sufe.edu.cn/info/303810); [Rodriguez, BEJ Theor. Econ. 2009](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:bpj:bejtec:v:9:y:2009:i:1:n:45)
- **[THEORY]** Ausubel, Cramton, Pycia, Rostek & Weretka (RES 81(4) 2014): in multi-unit auctions, bidders shade bids *differently across units* (demand reduction). This generically produces ex-post inefficient allocations in uniform-price and pay-as-bid formats. — [ACPRW, "Demand Reduction and Inefficiency in Multi-Unit Auctions"](https://www.econ.umd.edu/publication/demand-reduction-and-inefficiency-multi-unit-auctions)
- **[THEORY]** Benoît & Krishna: with budget-constrained bidders and sequential open ascending auctions, it is always revenue-optimal to sell the more valuable object first. The sequential format beats the simultaneous ascending auction when values differ a lot or when complementarities are significant. Budget constraints can arise endogenously as a strategic choice. — [Benoît & Krishna 1999 WP / RES 2001](https://econwpa.ub.uni-muenchen.de/econ-wp/game/papers/9805/9805001.pdf)
- **[THEORY]** Pitchik (GEB 2009), two budget-constrained bidders with incomplete information: multiple symmetric equilibria can differ in allocation, efficiency and revenue. "The sequence of sale affects the competition for a good" and hence prices, even when bidders far outnumber goods. If a good always goes to a strong bidder, its price and total revenue are higher when it is sold first. The mechanism is the option value of winning good 2 after good 1. — [Pitchik, "Budget-Constrained Sequential Auctions with Incomplete Information" (Toronto WP 342)](https://www.economics.utoronto.ca/public/workingPapers/tecipa-342.pdf)
- **[EMPIRICAL]** Pitchik & Schotter (RAND J. 19(3), 1988), lab experiments on budget-constrained sequential auctions with perfect information: budget limits change behavior, and the trembling-hand perfect equilibrium generally predicts prices well. Their 1986 theory shows a unique THP equilibrium amid many subgame-perfect ones. — [Pitchik & Schotter 1988 (RePEc)](https://ideas.repec.org/a/rje/randje/v19y1988iautumnp363-388.html)
- **[THEORY]** Ghosh & Liu: first-round aggressiveness falls with the option value of the later auction and rises with the chance of facing high-budget rivals. When competition dominates, sequential selling raises more expected revenue than a uniform-price auction. — [Ghosh & Liu 2019](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:gamebe:v:113:y:2019:i:c:p:611-632)

### Inferences
- **[INFERENCE] Option-value cap for duplicate letters.** Common letters (E, A, I, O, N, R, T, S) have many remaining copies. Weber-style logic says not to pay more for a copy now than the expected price of winning a later copy, plus a premium for the risk that the bot needs the letter *now* to complete a word before rivals or the game end. Implementable cap: `b ≤ E[min future clearing price of the same letter | copies_left, rounds_left] + urgency_premium`. For singletons (Q, Z, X, J, K) there is no option value, so bid the full b*.
- **[INFERENCE] Expected price path in this game.**
  - Early: complements (Menezes–Monteiro) and option value (Pitchik, Ghosh–Liu) argue for moderate early prices.
  - Mid-game: successful players' balances grow from word rewards, which pushes prices up.
  - End: zero terminal value of money makes bids all-in (Ghosh–Liu last-round rule), so prices spike to the second-highest balance.
  - Net prediction: rising prices late, which is the *opposite* of the declining price anomaly. Verify with logs (tag each clearing price with auction index and the sum of all balances).
- **[INFERENCE] Buy early vs late.** Buy early when (a) the tile is scarce or a singleton, (b) it completes or nearly completes a high-multiplier word, and (c) total opponent cash is low relative to its future level. Delay on plentiful letters. Hold enough cash late to outbid at least the second-richest rival for one critical finishing tile.
- **[INFERENCE] Order of sale is random here (bag draw), not chosen by a seller,** so the order-of-sale results matter only for prediction. A valuable tile appearing early is cheap relative to the same tile appearing late, when cash is abundant and nearly worthless.

### Gaps
- No source models sequential auctions where winnings refill budgets. The price-path prediction above is untested inference.
- The Lebrun (2024) and Weber (1983) primary texts were not read; the martingale claim rests on secondary citation.

## Q3. How should bidders handle complements and the exposure problem?

### Takeaway
Without package bidding, bidders for complements face exposure. Naive bidding either overpays and gets stuck with partial bundles, or under-bids and never completes bundles. Bidding the *standalone* marginal value of each item wins too many items for complements. What works is decision-theoretic: value each item as the expected surplus difference of *optimal future plans* with and without it, under a probabilistic price prediction, while treating already-won items as sunk. In sequential (as opposed to simultaneous) auctions, DP conditioning on current holdings handles exposure directly.

### Cited Findings
- **[THEORY/EMPIRICAL]** Bykowsky, Cull & Ledyard (J. Reg. Econ. 17(3), 2000): when license values have synergies, simple non-combinatorial auctions struggle to allocate efficiently. The difficulty grows with "fitting complexity", and bidding can become *mutually destructive*. Lab evidence shows combinatorial auctions beat simple auctions on efficiency and revenue when fitting complexity is low. — [Bykowsky, Cull, Ledyard (RePEc)](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:kap:regeco:v:17:y:2000:i:3:p:205-28); [Caltech WP](https://resolver.caltech.edu/CaltechAUTHORS:20170818-151840242)
- **[THEORY]** Goeree & Lien (JET 2014), equilibrium of the simultaneous ascending auction with local (single-item) and global (multi-item) bidders: global bidders face an exposure problem that lowers revenue and efficiency, and it worsens when items are substitutes for locals. Medium-sized global bidders split the market at low prices when sharing is feasible, but bid mutually destructively when they face a fitting problem. — [Goeree & Lien, "An equilibrium analysis of the simultaneous ascending auction"](https://academicnewsletter.sufe.edu.cn/info/300541)
- **[EMPIRICAL]** Brunner, Goeree, Holt & Ledyard (2010): package bidding improves performance in lab spectrum auctions when value complementarities are present. — [typeset copy](https://typeset.io/pdf/an-experimental-test-of-flexible-combinatorial-spectrum-1ay937xsft.pdf)
- **[THEORY]** Boutilier, Goldszmidt & Sabata (IJCAI 1999): agents bid for needed resources one at a time in sequence instead of on bundles. This avoids revealing bundle preferences and limits exposure when bids cannot be retracted. A dynamic-programming model computes bidding policies from estimated distributions of winning bids, which are updated over time (learning). — [Boutilier et al., "Sequential Auctions for the Allocation of Resources with Complementarities"](https://www.cs.toronto.edu/~cebly/Papers/auctions-abs.html)
- **[THEORY]** Exposure defined: "an active agent desiring a bundle of goods runs the risk that it will purchase some but not all goods in the bundle." Straightforward bidding (SB) is oblivious to exposure and can leave an agent "stuck with a useless good" at negative surplus. — [Osepayshvili, Wellman, Reeves, MacKie-Mason, "Self-Confirming Price Prediction for Bidding in Simultaneous Ascending Auctions" (UAI 2005)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1207.1400)
- **[THEORY]** Osepayshvili et al.'s *sunk-aware* distribution predictor sets the expected incremental price for a good the agent is *not* winning to Δ^L = E_F[p | p ≥ β+1]. For a good it *is* winning, Δ^W = (1 − Pr(β|β))·E_F[p | p ≥ β+2], because the current bid is sunk unless outbid. The agent then picks the best bundle at these perceived prices. — [Osepayshvili et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1207.1400)
- **[THEORY]** Marginal value at prices: µ(x_j, p) = σ*(p[p_j←0]) − σ*(p[p_j←∞]), meaning optimal surplus with good j free minus optimal surplus with good j unavailable. Bidding this for every good is the "StraightMV" heuristic. — [Wellman, Sodomka & Greenwald, "Self-confirming price-prediction strategies for simultaneous one-shot auctions" (GEB 102, 2017; arXiv 1210.4915)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1210.4915)
- **[THEORY]** Greenwald & Boyan show by example that marginal-utility bidding is not optimal in simultaneous auctions, even in deterministic settings. Greenwald's summary: for complements, marginal-value bidding tends to win *too many* goods (exposure); for substitutes, too few. — [Greenwald & Boyan, "Bidding under Uncertainty: Theory and Experiments" (UAI 2004)](https://cs.brown.edu/people/amy/papers/abs/uai.html); [Greenwald slides](https://cs.brown.edu/people/amy/papers/nyu.pdf)
- **[EMPIRICAL]** In Wellman–Sodomka–Greenwald's empirical game analysis, explicit bid optimizers given a price distribution (LocalBid search, BidEval candidate evaluation) dominate marginal-value heuristics. AverageMU64 did well in complement environments but "lacks robustness across valuation classes". — [Wellman, Sodomka, Greenwald](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1210.4915)
- **[THEORY]** For a global bidder against local bidders in simultaneous Vickrey auctions of *perfect substitutes*, the global bidder should bid non-zero in all auctions: high in one and equal or lower in the others. The analysis extends to sequential-plus-simultaneous markets. — [Gerding, Dash, Byde, Jennings, JAIR 32 (2008)](https://eprints.soton.ac.uk/263267)

### Inferences
- **[INFERENCE] Exposure in this game is per-word but softened by flexibility.** A tile's value should be the expected score gain across *all* words it could help form with the current rack, the tiles still to come, and the remaining time. It should not be tied to one target word. High-flexibility tiles (E, S, R, A) carry low exposure. Q, J, X and Z carry high exposure. Valuing a tile against one target word reproduces the "win too many complements" failure of standalone marginal value.
- **[INFERENCE] Sunk-awareness carries over directly.** Money already spent on held tiles is sunk. The bid for the next tile should depend only on forward expected score with and without it (the W difference in Q1). This raises bids on the *completing* tile, sometimes above its standalone share of the word's reward. That is correct: once the first k letters are owned, the (k+1)-th captures the whole remaining word value.
- **[INFERENCE] Mutually destructive bidding risk.** Goeree–Lien's fitting problem applies when two or more bots chase the same scarce letter for different high-multiplier words. Unchecked, both bid up to their full completion value. Track which rivals' racks plausibly need which letters, and avoid escalating on contested scarce letters when an alternative word path exists.
- **[INFERENCE] Compute.** Use a precomputed index from sorted letter multisets to words (anagram dictionary) and enumerate words reachable from rack ∪ {x} ∪ (sampled future tiles). That costs O(S × subsets) per auction, which is feasible within ~100–300 ms for S ≈ 50–200 samples if racks are small (≤ 12 tiles).

### Gaps
- Goeree–Lien and Bykowsky et al. full texts were not read; findings rest on abstracts.
- No source quantifies exposure costs in *sequential sealed-bid* auctions of letter-like items specifically.

## Q4. What did winning Trading Agent Competition agents actually do, and what worked measurably?

### Takeaway
The winning recipe repeated across TAC Travel, SCM, AA and Power TAC: (1) a *probabilistic* price or acceptance model, learned from data or derived from a competitive-equilibrium simulation, plus (2) explicit optimization of bids against sampled scenarios (SAA, sampled expected marginal values, or a small MDP). Marginal-value heuristics lost to explicit optimizers in controlled studies. Pitfalls were also measured: too few samples truncate the price tail, and clairvoyant per-scenario planning overestimates value.

### Cited Findings
- **[EMPIRICAL] RoxyBot-06 (TAC Travel 2006 champion).**
  - Hotel prices were predicted by simulating simultaneous ascending auctions (SimAA) to get a *distribution* of competitive-equilibrium prices.
  - Bids were chosen by **Sample Average Approximation**: sample S scenarios, then pick the bid vector maximizing the *sum over scenarios* of optimal-plan surplus (Definition 5.8). This was contrasted with the Expected Value Method, which optimizes against the average scenario.
  - Final scores: RoxyBot 4032, Walverine 4032, WhiteDolphin 3936, 006 3902, Mertacor 3880. RoxyBot won on the control-variate-adjusted score.
  - In 1000-game controlled experiments, SAA variants (SAAB, SAAT) were the best agents in the game-theoretic setting and among the best in the decision-theoretic one. They beat marginal-utility heuristics SMU, AMU, TMU, TMU*, BE and BE*.
  - SimAA took ~0.1–0.2 s per sample vs ~1.1–2.2 s for tâtonnement.
  - [Greenwald, Lee & Naroditskiy, "RoxyBot-06: Stochastic Prediction and Optimization in TAC Travel", JAIR 36 (2009)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1401.3829)
- **[THEORY/EMPIRICAL] SAA tail-underbidding fix.** SAA never bids above its highest sampled price. Under perfect prediction, the chance that all S samples fall below the clearing price is 1/(S+1). With S = 49 and 8 independent hotels, the chance that at least one hotel is unwinnable is about 0.15. **SAA\*** replaces |N| samples with hard-coded scenarios that set each good to its upper price limit, with the others at their means. — [RoxyBot-06](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1401.3829)
- **[EMPIRICAL] RoxyBot vs rivals.** No single metric explains the win: RoxyBot balanced "contradictory goals" (high hotel cost, mid flight cost, good bonuses). Walverine bid lower prices on fewer hotels yet won more and wasted less. — [RoxyBot-06](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1401.3829)
- **[EMPIRICAL] ATTac-2001 (top scorer, TAC-01).**
  - Hotel price distributions were learned with boosting-based conditional density estimation.
  - For each open hotel, ATTac assumed it closes next, sampled closing prices of the others, solved the allocation (LP relaxation) with and without i copies, and **bid the mean of V_i − V_{i−1} across samples** (expected marginal value). It used the full minute between closings to draw as many samples as possible.
  - The Vickrey-optimal bid is "expected final score assuming one owns the item minus expected final score assuming one does not".
  - [Stone, Schapire, Littman, Csirik & McAllester, "Decision-Theoretic Bidding Based on Learned Density Models in Simultaneous, Interacting Auctions", JAIR 19 (2003)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1106.5270.pdf); [Schapire et al., ICML 2002](https://mlanthology.org/icml/2002/schapire2002icml-modeling/)
- **[EMPIRICAL] ATTac caveat.** In controlled experiments, *using expected hotel prices instead of price samples "consistently performs better"*, which surprised the authors. Their suggested causes: too few samples (high variance), and per-sample planning "assumes the agent can behave with perfect knowledge for each individual sample", which overestimates score. Sampling seemed most useful for hotel decisions and neutral for flights and entertainment. — [Stone et al. JAIR 2003](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1106.5270.pdf)
- **[EMPIRICAL] Walverine / TAC-02 price-prediction survey.** Walverine predicted hotel prices as the Walrasian competitive equilibrium (by tâtonnement), treating opponents as price-takers. Across TAC-02 entrants, flight-price-aware prediction was the key differentiator. ML methods learned the flight-to-hotel relationship, and "a purely analytical approach based on competitive equilibrium analysis achieves equal accuracy with no historical data". Walverine was best on EVPP (expected value of perfect prediction, p ≈ .01). Only Walverine and ATTac01 beat the best constant predictor on EVPP. — [Wellman, Reeves, Lochner, Vorobeychik, "Price Prediction in a Trading Agent Competition", JAIR 21 (2004)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1107.0034)
- **[EMPIRICAL] Self-confirming price prediction (SCPP).**
  - Self-confirming distributions are found by iterating: simulate all agents playing PP(F^k), record prices, set F^{k+1} to the empirical prices, and stop when Kolmogorov–Smirnov distance < 0.01. This took 6 iterations in a 5-agent/5-slot case, ≤ 11 for 10 uniform instances and ≤ 7 for 11 exponential ones, at 10^6 games per iteration.
  - In empirical game analysis over 53 strategies, PP(F^SC) was the unique surviving strategy (unique Nash equilibrium) in every maximal clique tested. Self-confirming distributions cannot always exist (a no-competitive-equilibrium counterexample).
  - [Osepayshvili et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1207.1400)
- **[THEORY/EMPIRICAL] One-shot SCPP.** Bayes-Nash equilibrium in independent-private-value simultaneous one-shot auctions can be characterized as optimal price-prediction strategies with self-confirming predictions. Top strategies built predictions on the **highest-other-bid (HB) statistic rather than the transaction price**, which "is aligned with what the theory would dictate". — [Wellman, Sodomka, Greenwald (GEB 2017)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1210.4915)
- **[EMPIRICAL] Sequential multi-unit extension.** Greenwald, Li & Sodomka split strategy into prediction (best-reply dynamics produce "stable price predictions") and optimization (solve the induced MDP with Monte Carlo). The method recovered known analytical equilibria and found new approximate equilibria in sequential auctions with multi-unit demand. — [Greenwald, Li, Sodomka, NeurIPS 2012](https://papers.nips.cc/paper_files/paper/2012/hash/801c14f07f9724229175b8ef8b4585a8-Abstract.html)
- **[EMPIRICAL] TacTex'13 (Power TAC 2013 champion), wholesale bidding.**
  - The MDP has states s ∈ {24…0, success} (hours to delivery) and actions that are limit prices. The value is solved by one backward sweep: V(s) = min_price [p_cleared·price + (1−p_cleared)·V(s−1)].
  - p_cleared(s, price) is estimated nonparametrically from all past trades in state s, reusing data across bidding processes (a modified Tesauro sequential-bidding algorithm). It falls back to random bidding if fewer than 6 trades are recorded.
  - Ablation: the full agent scored 389.9 vs 182.8 with randomized wholesale bidding.
  - [Urieli & Stone, "TacTex'13: A Champion Adaptive Power Trading Agent", AAAI 2014](https://object.cloud.sdsc.edu/v1/AUTH_da4962d3368042ac8337e2dfdd3e7bf3/ml-papers-txt/AAAI/2014/tactex13_a_champion_adaptive_power_trading_agent__0db84573.txt)
- **[EMPIRICAL] TacTex-05 (TAC SCM 2005 champion).** An Offer Acceptance Predictor maps each candidate offer price to an acceptance probability using past bidding results, alongside supplier and demand models. — [Pardoe & Stone, "TacTex-05: A Champion Supply Chain Management Agent", AAAI 2006](https://cdn.aaai.org/AAAI/2006/AAAI06-234.pdf)
- **[EMPIRICAL] TAC Ad Auctions.** The 2009 winner TacTex worked by "estimating the full game". Brown's analysis splits agents into modeling and optimization parts. Rule-based optimizers tolerate inaccurate models, while greedy multiple-choice knapsack optimizers decide better but need more accurate models. — [Pardoe et al., AAMAS 2010](https://www.cs.utexas.edu/~dpardoe/papers/AAMAS10.pdf); [Brown TADA 2010](https://cs.uwaterloo.ca/conferences/tada2010/Brown.pdf)
- **[EMPIRICAL]** WhiteBear (top TAC-02 scorer) came from a principled study of design tradeoffs, including how aggressive bids should be versus the cost of buying flexibility. A strategy derived from Bayes-Nash analysis was later used "very successfully" by WhiteBear in TAC-05. — [Vetsikas & Selman, AAMAS 2003 / Vetsikas, Jennings, Selman IJCAI 2007](https://eprints.soton.ac.uk/264226/1/ijcai07-vetsikas.pdf)

### Inferences
- **[INFERENCE] Direct transplant: an ATTac/RoxyBot-style evaluator for each tile.**
  1. Sample S futures: remaining bag order (from known unsold tile counts), opponent highest-bid draws per future auction (from the learned HB distribution), and word-reward timing.
  2. For each sample, run a *fast greedy policy* (not a clairvoyant optimum) for "win x" and "lose x".
  3. Bid the mean score difference converted at α_t, or solve the indifference price directly.
  Using a non-clairvoyant policy inside samples addresses ATTac's overestimation finding.
- **[INFERENCE]** Add **SAA\*-style tail scenarios**, with a rival bidding its full balance, so the bot does not systematically under-bid on critical singletons.
- **[INFERENCE]** Model the **highest-opponent-bid distribution** per (letter, auction index, max rival balance), not just observed clearing prices. In a second-price auction, the clearing price you observe when you win *is* the highest other bid. When you lose you see only a lower bound on the winner's bid, so treat losses as censored observations.
- **[INFERENCE]** TacTex's nonparametric per-state win-probability table is cheap and directly usable: key on (letter class, rounds_left bucket, rival-cash bucket) and pool data across games.
- **[INFERENCE] Compute.** SAA with S ≈ 50–100 and greedy rollouts is the heaviest viable option at one auction per second. EVM (one average scenario) is roughly S× cheaper and, per ATTac, sometimes *better*. A/B test both.

### Gaps
- The full TacTex-05 SCM and TAC AA papers were not read for quantitative ablations; only champion status and model structure are sourced.
- The *Autonomous Bidding Agents* book (Wellman, Greenwald & Stone, MIT Press 2007) was not accessed directly. Its content is represented through the JAIR papers above.

## Q5. Dynamic programming / MDP formulations of sequential bidding with a budget: what state variables matter, and what approximations are tractable within a sub-second deadline?

### Takeaway
The minimal sufficient state is (own money, own holdings, auctions remaining), plus a compressed summary of opponents (their money, and at most a coarse guess of their needs). Optimal bids come from value-function differences. Tractable approximations in rising cost order:
1. Lagrangian pacing (O(1) per bid).
2. 1-D value function in money × time, precomputed offline (O(grid)).
3. Nonparametric clearing-probability tables with backward induction (TacTex).
4. Monte Carlo / SAA rollouts (O(S·T·wordgen)).
5. Offline self-confirming or best-reply iteration to fit opponent price models.

### Cited Findings
- **[THEORY]** Gummadi et al.: state (b, v); Bellman V(b) = E_v max_{u≤b} E_{b0}[1{u>b0}(v − b0 + e^{−β}V(b+a−b0)) + 1{u≤b0} e^{−β}V(b+a)]. As β → 0 the value function has a limit given by a differential equation that "can be solved efficiently", and the bid is v/(1+V′(B)). They also give a finite-horizon, undiscounted version. — [Gummadi et al.](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2016/02/SSRN-id2066175.pdf)
- **[THEORY/EMPIRICAL]** Cai et al.: state (t, b, x). V(t,b) is solved by DP at small scale and approximated by neural networks at large scale. The bid policy depends on V only through the difference V(t−1, b−δ) − V(t−1, b). — [Cai et al. 2017](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1701.02490)
- **[THEORY]** Stone et al. write the exact value as nested expectations over future closing prices, value(i, H) = max_{r_i} E_{y_i} … E_{y_n}[v(G+H) − G·Y], which is exponential. They approximate it by sampling price vectors (Kearns–Mansour–Ng sparse sampling) and by reordering max and expectation. — [Stone et al. JAIR 2003](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1106.5270.pdf)
- **[THEORY]** Boutilier et al. use DP over sequential auctions with complementarities, with learned winning-bid distributions. A UAI-99 follow-up uses continuous value-function approximation for policies with complements and substitutes. — [Boutilier et al. IJCAI 1999](https://www.cs.toronto.edu/~cebly/Papers/auctions-abs.html); [Continuous VFA](https://www.cs.toronto.edu/~cebly/Papers/contauct-abs.html)
- **[THEORY]** A DP and value-iteration formulation of sequential auctions with quasi-linear utility and budget constraints exists. Its authors note optimality is not guaranteed unless other agents bid in the same style. — [arXiv 1301.2276](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1301.2276)
- **[EMPIRICAL]** TacTex'13's acyclic 25-state MDP is solved in a single backward pass every timeslot, with transition probabilities estimated online from pooled trade data. — [Urieli & Stone 2014](https://object.cloud.sdsc.edu/v1/AUTH_da4962d3368042ac8337e2dfdd3e7bf3/ml-papers-txt/AAAI/2014/tactex13_a_champion_adaptive_power_trading_agent__0db84573.txt)
- **[THEORY]** Dual-gradient pacing (Balseiro & Gur): bid b_j = v_j/(1+α_j), with α_{j+1} = max(α_j + η(B/m − p_j), 0) and η ≈ m^{−1/2}. It is self-correcting, holding spend near B/m per auction, with O(√m) regret against the offline optimum under i.i.d. inputs. — [Aggarwal et al. survey](https://sigecom.org/exchanges/volume_22/1/AGGARWAL.pdf); [Balseiro & Gur, Mgmt Sci 65(9) 2019](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:inm:ormnsc:v:65:y:2019:i:9:p:3952-3968)

### Inferences
- **[INFERENCE] State variables that matter for this game (priority order).**
  1. Own money m.
  2. Own tile multiset H, compressed to features: best currently playable word score, best "one-tile-away" completions and their rewards, vowel/consonant balance.
  3. Auctions remaining T and the remaining bag composition (counts per letter, inferable from tiles already sold plus the known distribution).
  4. Rivals' money vector, or at least max and second-max. This decides endgame auctions and sets the price level.
  5. Rivals' observable holdings, for contested-letter detection.
  6. Learned per-letter HB distributions.
  Raw holdings are too large for tabular DP, so factor W(m, H, T) ≈ S_now(H) + Φ(H, T) + α(m, T)·m, or learn W by regression from self-play logs.
- **[INFERENCE] Tier 1 (≤ 1 ms): pacing multiplier.** b = min(m, ΔS(x)·k_t), with k_t = 1/α_t. Set the target spend rate so m reaches ~0 at game end; leftover money is waste. Update k_t ← k_t·exp(η·(target_spend_to_date − actual_spend_to_date)/scale), clipped to [k_min, k_max], and force k_t → ∞ (all-in) in the last few auctions. This is the value-maximizer analogue of Balseiro–Gur dual descent.
- **[INFERENCE] Tier 2 (≤ 10 ms): money×time value function.** Offline, fit α(m, T) = ∂W/∂m from simulations (e.g., a lookup grid of 101 money × 70 time cells), then use the indifference-price form. Gummadi's "shading grows as balance drops" should emerge naturally.
- **[INFERENCE] Tier 3 (≤ 300 ms): SAA rollouts.** For each candidate outcome (win or lose x), run S ≈ 30–100 rollouts of the remaining auctions with a fast greedy bot policy and sampled rival HB bids. Score each by the final score of the bot (or the bot-minus-best-rival margin), and bid the indifference price via bisection over δ, reusing common random numbers across δ values.
- **[INFERENCE] Tier 4 (offline): self-confirming loop.** Run 6 copies of the bot (plus scripted variants), fit HB distributions, re-run, and stop when KS < 0.01. Per Osepayshvili et al. this converges within ~6–11 iterations when it converges at all. Keep a smoothing fallback for oscillation.
- **[INFERENCE] Objective choice.** Since "highest score wins", optimize P(rank 1) or E[margin over best rival] rather than E[own score] in rollouts. This naturally produces risk-seeking when behind and risk-aversion when ahead.

### Gaps
- No found paper solves a sequential budgeted auction where the items are inputs to a combinatorial word-formation step. The factorization of W above is untested.
- Cai et al.'s neural V approximation details and Gummadi's limiting ODE were not extracted in full.

## Q6. What is known about auto-bidding equilibria when many players use the same pacing or learning rule?

### Takeaway
When all bidders use gradient-based pacing in second-price auctions, the dynamics are guaranteed good in aggregate: liquid welfare is at least half of optimal even *without* convergence. Convergence to Nash is proven only in large-market regimes (many auctions and competitors). Computing a pacing equilibrium in second-price auctions is PPAD-complete, and multiple equilibria with very different outcomes exist. With 6 bidders and ~68 items, the large-market regime does not hold. Expect oscillation, ties among identical bots, and exploitable predictability.

### Cited Findings
- **[THEORY]** Balseiro & Gur (Mgmt Sci 65(9) 2019): adaptive pacing strategies are asymptotically optimal when competitors' bids are i.i.d. and also when they are arbitrary. When *all* bidders use them, the dynamics converge, and the strategies form an approximate Nash equilibrium in a regime where unilateral deviation gains vanish as the number of auctions and competitors grows. — [Stanford GSB listing](https://www.gsb.stanford.edu/faculty-research/publications/learning-repeated-auctions-budgets-regret-minimization-equilibrium)
- **[THEORY]** Gaitonde, Li, Light, Lucier & Slivkins: when all agents use gradient-based pacing, liquid welfare over the dynamics is ≥ 1/2 of optimal expected liquid welfare, "without requiring convergence". This holds for any core auction (first-price, second-price, GSP) and is robust to correlated values. Individual dynamic-regret bounds cover both utility and value maximizers. They note that finding a pure Nash equilibrium of the pacing game is PPAD-hard for second-price. — [arXiv 2205.08674](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.08674)
- **[THEORY]** Chen, Kroer & Kumar (Math. OR 49(4) 2024): finding an approximate pacing equilibrium in second-price auctions is PPAD-complete. This answers Conitzer et al.'s conjecture and implies that Borgs et al.'s tâtonnement-style budget dynamics are unlikely to converge efficiently. A follow-up shows γ-approximate pacing equilibrium is PPAD-hard for any constant γ < 1/3, but polynomial with few buyers. — [arXiv 2103.13969](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2103.13969); [INFORMS](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/moor.2022.0009)
- **[THEORY]** Conitzer, Kroer, Sodomka & Stier-Moses (Oper. Res. 70(2) 2022): pacing equilibria always exist and refine competitive equilibria. There can be *multiple* pacing equilibria with large variation in natural objectives, and finding welfare- or revenue-maximizing ones is NP-hard. — [arXiv 1706.07151](https://arxiv.org/abs/1706.07151v3)
- **[THEORY]** For value-maximizing autobidders using uniform (dual-based) bidding in second-price auctions, the price of anarchy is ≤ 2 (liquid welfare), and the bound is tight even with 2 bidders. Uniform bid scaling is near-optimal for bidders in truthful auctions. — [Aggarwal et al. survey, §4.3](https://sigecom.org/exchanges/volume_22/1/AGGARWAL.pdf)
- **[THEORY]** Gummadi et al. prove existence of mean-field equilibria for repeated second-price auctions with budgets when there are many bidders. — [Gummadi et al.](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2016/02/SSRN-id2066175.pdf)
- **[EMPIRICAL]** In SCPP empirical game analysis, the all-PP(F^SC) profile was stable. The best deviation among 26 alternative strategies gained little, and all mixtures of other strategy pairs had ε ≥ 0.32 against a baseline payoff of 4.51. Strategic stability therefore comes from *predictions consistent with everyone's actual strategy*. — [Osepayshvili et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1207.1400)

### Inferences
- **[INFERENCE] If all 6 bots use the same pacing rule,** symmetric multipliers produce near-identical bids on popular letters. Second-price then pays the winner the near-identical runner-up bid, so prices approach the common valuation, rents are dissipated, and ties are frequent. Add small randomized tie-breaking jitter. Exploit differences in *valuation*, not multiplier: a better word engine yields higher ΔS on the tiles that matter.
- **[INFERENCE] Exploiting predictable pacers.** A rival using dual-gradient pacing reveals its multiplier through its bid/price history. Estimate it online and predict its future HB. Benoît–Krishna's raise-rivals'-costs logic applies: bid near (but below) your indifference price on letters a pacing rival wants, so its spend jumps and its multiplier drops in later auctions.
- **[INFERENCE] Do not rely on convergence.** Six bidders and about 68 auctions put the game far outside the large-market asymptotics of Balseiro–Gur and the mean-field results. Gaitonde et al.'s non-convergent welfare guarantee is aggregate, not individual. Use the self-confirming loop (Q4/Q5) against the *actual* opponent pool, refreshed from live logs.

### Gaps
- No found result covers pacing equilibria for value maximizers whose budgets grow endogenously from wins (reward-as-currency), or for small numbers of bidders with relative (rank) objectives.
- Thoma, Bosshard & Seuken and other recent RL/NE-computation work for sequential auctions were not reviewed.
