# Live match data analysis: what winning looks like in recorded ScrabbleBot matches

Scope and provenance (applies to every section below):
- Dataset: union of `data/learning/matches/*.json` (359), the 89 extra matches in `data/learning/history.json`, and 9 matches present only in `data/learning/self/` = 457 matches, 26 Oct-7 Nov 2026 window (first match 2026-10-24, last 2026-11-07). 455 are "standard" (6 seats, 68 auctions, rack 5, 1000 ms Vickrey) and are used for all statistics; 2 non-standard matches (4 and 7 seats) are excluded. 9 matches lack `topBid` and drop out of bid-level analyses. Sources: [matches](data/learning/matches/), [history.json](data/learning/history.json), [self](data/learning/self/).
- Data integrity checks, all passed: score = sum of word rewards for 2153/2153 archive participants; ending balance = 100 - total paid + total rewards for 2153/2153; reward = floor(sum(tile values) x length multiplier) for 5480/5480 archived plays; every played word is in `data/enable.txt`; reconstructed rack size never went negative; no bot used more than 5 non-purchased (starting) tiles; `topBid` never exceeded the winner's reconstructed balance; `topBid >= paid` in all 24,417 archived auctions. Source: [build.py](/Users/pomoika/Documents/GitHub_repo/scrabble/research_notes/ScrabbleBot auction strategy research/scripts/build.py), [v1.py](/Users/pomoika/Documents/GitHub_repo/scrabble/research_notes/ScrabbleBot auction strategy research/scripts/v1.py).
- `topBid` is the winning bid (published after the match), `paid` is the second-highest bid (Vickrey price). So the winner's exact bid is observed, and for any other seat the rival top bid is observed: `paid` if that seat won, otherwise `topBid`. Only losing bids below second place are unobserved.
- Selection bias: matches were collected as the recent and stratified older games of the top three bots plus all of ours ([collect-opponents.ts](scripts/collect-opponents.ts)). 452/455 standard matches contain at least one of LUCENT/Bron/Waupus. Results describe leader tables, not the whole population.
- Regime shift: 194 matches are from Oct 24-30 and 261 from Oct 31-Nov 7. In the later period 48.1% of seats were "inactive" (fewer than 6 tiles bought; e.g. bots 1-6, 63, 145, 165, 169), compared with 9.5% earlier. Average paid price fell from about 13.3 to about 8-9 per tile at the same date. Several results differ by period and are split where it matters.
- Accounting identity: for every seat, final score = total spent + ending balance - 100 (holds for 100% of seats). Money has no terminal value. Spending converts money into score, and rewards are returned as cash.
- Phases used throughout: auctions with remaining 67-51, 50-34, 33-17, 16-1, and the final tile (remaining 0).

## Price curve: clearing price by letter and by auction position

### Takeaway
The Vickrey clearing price is flat across the game at about 10.5-11 per tile. There is no declining-price pattern. Winning top bids rise sharply in the last 16 auctions (mean 18.8 vs about 14). Prices are set mainly by face value (vowels and 1-point consonants about 8-9; JQXZ 24-30) and by how many active bidders sit at the table. High confidence for the descriptive numbers.

### Inferences
- There is no cheap phase to wait for. The second price is the same throughout. The final 16 auctions are where overbidding happens (top bids rise while value per tile falls; see the next section).
- The final tile is unplayable: no play occurred after the last auction in any match. Any bid on it is pure waste, apart from denying an opponent nothing.
- Calibrate on table activity, not on letter alone. At our tables a bid of 10-12 on a vowel or LNRST tile wins only about 16-32% of the time.

### Gaps
- Losing bids below second place are not observed, so the full demand curve is unknown. Only the top two bids per auction are visible.
- The Oct 31 price drop coincides with a jump in inactive seats. The data cannot separate a server or lobby change from changes in the bots' policies.

## Letter value: realized reward of bought tiles vs price paid

### Takeaway
Bought tiles are almost always played (94.5%). On average each returns about 4.6 points of reward against 10.8 paid, so every letter is "overpriced" in cash terms. That is expected, because money has no terminal value. The right comparison is score per coin. On that measure, which letters look best depends on the attribution method: Shapley shares favor vowels, while within-match regression favors high-value letters. Tiles bought late are clearly overpriced relative to early ones. Medium confidence.

### Inferences
- In cash terms every letter is "overpriced" (paid > reward), so "underpriced" has to mean more score per coin than the alternatives.
- The regression is the closest to a marginal value because it compares buying one more tile of a group with not buying it. It says 1-point consonants (LNRST, 0.45 points per coin) and vowels (0.51) are the worst value per coin, and high-value letters bought by the bots who chose them return 0.62-0.70.
- Shapley says the opposite, because in a single 7-letter word every letter is essential. The truth for a bid rule is rack-conditional: a Z is worth roughly its face value times the multiplier only if the rack can use it.
- Phase is the robust finding. A late tile costs the same but returns about 40% less (3.2 vs 5.5) and goes unplayed 14% of the time. Late bids should be discounted unless the tile completes a known word.
- Bron gets nearly LUCENT's value per tile while paying 8.29 vs 10.28. Our tiles cost the most (12.10) and return the least (4.16).

### Gaps
- No clean causal value per letter exists, because buyers select letters that fit their racks (selection bias). The regression may also partly reflect bot skill.
- First-in-first-out assignment of bought vs starting tiles is ambiguous when a bot's starting rack holds the same letter. The starting letters of other bots are unknown, except that the table-wide multiset is known (bag minus auctioned letters).

## Winner analysis: which features predict final score and winning

### Takeaway
Winning is overwhelmingly about converting money into many tiles and recycling rewards quickly. Winners buy 22.4 tiles vs 9.1 for the other seats, and tile count alone explains 78% of within-match score variance among active bots. Average price paid barely matters once tile count is fixed. Heavy spending in the first quartile is negative and late-quartile spending is positive. Winners earn about 0.65 points per coin spent vs 0.51 for the rest. High confidence on direction; effect sizes are correlational and partly mechanical, because score = spend + end balance - 100.

### Inferences
- Treat the game as a money-velocity problem. Each coin spent returns about 0.5-0.7 points, and those points come back as cash that can be spent again. Total score scales roughly with points per coin / (1 - points per coin), so moving from 0.5 to 0.65 nearly doubles the multiplier (1.0 to 1.86).
- A bid rule should keep buying throughout the game rather than front-load. Being out-bid on price is cheap; owning too few tiles is fatal. The evidence: avg price has no independent effect on winning, and Q1-heavy spending hurts.
- Our gap is mostly volume (12.4 vs 22.4 tiles) plus short, early words. That lowers points per coin (0.49) and shrinks the cash we recycle.

### Gaps
- All estimates are observational. The tiles-bought effect is partly mechanical through the accounting identity and the reward-to-cash feedback, and confounded by bot quality.
- Without losing-bid data, we cannot measure how much bot quality comes from bidding vs word search.

## Reconstructing LUCENT (18) and Bron (114) policies

### Takeaway
Both leaders hold to a rack of about 8-9 tiles and play a 7+ letter word about 70% of the time, usually within 0.2 s of buying the completing tile. In the last 16 auctions they liquidate with shorter words. Bron bids broadly but cheaply early, then buys the most tiles late. LUCENT is selective, often not bidding even at low prices, but bids very high (90th percentile 20-48) when it wants a tile. Neither has a vowel/consonant bias beyond supply. LUCENT spends down (median ending balance 22); Bron does not (43). Medium-high confidence, based on 252 LUCENT and 353 Bron active seats.

### Inferences
- LUCENT's pattern is a completion-driven bidder: no or low bids on most tiles, and high bids (often 1.5-2x the market) on tiles that complete or nearly complete a long word. It then plays immediately.
- Bron's pattern is a broad low bidder: about 7 on 1-point letters and about 11-15 on mid letters through the middle game, and it buys the most tiles in the last 16 auctions with bids around 11-15. Its large unspent balance (mean 52.5) suggests money left on the table even for a top bot.
- Both hold racks of 8-9 tiles, which is enough to form a 7-letter word with spare tiles (a 7-tile random rack forms a 7-letter word only 7.3% of the time; 8 tiles 27.4%; see the next section). They switch to short-word liquidation only in the last 16 auctions.
- Our profile is the opposite: early play (7 s), small racks (median 6), 46% short words, front-loaded buying, and almost no late buying.

### Gaps
- Bid curves assume a leader's bid is independent of the rival top bid. That is violated when several bots chase the same completion, so the curves are approximate.
- Bot versions change over time (`versionStartedAt` for LUCENT is about Oct 25), and no split by version was done here.

## Which 7+ letter words top bots play, and which letters reach them

### Takeaway
7+ letter words are 53% of plays but 77% of all reward (mean 41.6 vs 13.4 points). Top bots almost never repeat a word: 1,717 unique words in 1,877 plays. ## Counterfactual replay: what a simple per-letter cap would have bought

### Takeaway
Using only recorded prices, a flat cap of 8-10 would have won far too few tiles to compete: 9-15 tiles, 42-63 points, rank about 5 of 7. Caps of 12-20 plateau at about 75-80 points, around our actual level and far below winners (about 150). The naive replay that assumes you pay the recorded clearing price overstates results by 40-110%. Low-medium confidence: it ignores price impact and opponent reactions.

### Inferences
- A non-selective cap caps out at about 18 tiles and about 4.2 points per tile, because it buys whatever is cheap rather than what completes words. Winners get about 8 points per tile, counting starting tiles.
- The lever is rack-conditional valuation: bid well above market (16-25 for normal letters) on tiles that complete a 7+ letter word, and the market price or less otherwise. That is what LUCENT appears to do.
- Calibrate to the price to beat (`topBid`), not to `paid`. A rule tuned on clearing prices will badly overestimate how many tiles it can win.
- Among the simple rules, letter-scaled caps around 13 (1-point letters) / 15 (DG) / 18 (mid) / 21 (K) / 31 (JQXZ), combined with hold-for-7, gave the best phantom result. Even that is only about our current level without completion-aware bidding.

### Gaps
- No price impact: the displaced winners would have bought other tiles and bid differently.
- The phantom's word policy is a simple greedy one, so a better planner would score more.
- The exact own-seat replay (using our known start racks from `bidTrace` and rival top prices) was not run. With only 19 traced matches it would be low-power anyway.

## Table effects: strong and active bots vs prices and winner scores

### Takeaway
The number of active bidders, not the number of leaders, drives prices. Each extra active bot adds about 1.7 to the mean clearing price and lowers the winner's score by about 35. Each extra leader adds about 0.6 to price and costs the winner about 15 points. Our tables are slightly more competitive than average. High confidence for direction.

### Inferences
- A bid rule should read table activity: the count of seats that have bought tiles so far, or their balances. Shift price targets by about +1.7 per active opponent, using the price-to-beat tables in the price-curve section.
- At quiet tables (2-3 active), cheap broad buying wins very large scores. At full tables, everyone's score compresses to about 80-115 and leaders win only 15-27% of the time.

### Gaps
- "Active" is defined after the fact (6+ tiles bought), which is partly an outcome. A live rule would need to use activity observed so far in the match.
- Leaders' and others' policies may adapt to table composition; the data cannot separate this from mechanical competition.
