# Budget pacing and bid optimization in real-time ad auctions (applied to a 68-round, 6-player, replenishable-budget tile auction)

Evidence labels used below: **[THEORY]** proven result; **[SIM]** simulation or offline replay of logged data; **[PROD]** live online A/B test or production deployment; **[GUIDE]** practitioner guidance without a controlled experiment.

Game context, as given by the coordinator (not independently verified): about 68 sequential sealed-bid second-price auctions, one per second; 6 players; starting cash 100; income arrives mid-game when words are played. Our bot buys about 80% of its tiles in the first half at an average price of 13.3. Top rivals pay 7.8–9.6 per tile and buy about 25 tiles, spread evenly.

## Q1. Exact form of adaptive pacing / dual-gradient bidding (Balseiro & Gur; Balseiro, Lu, Mirrokni dual mirror descent): what bid = v/(1+mu) means, how mu is updated, and what guarantees hold at ~68 rounds

### Takeaway
Adaptive pacing bids `b_t = min(v_t/(1+mu_t), cash_t)`. It then takes a projected subgradient step on the budget's dual price: `mu_{t+1} = clip(mu_t - eps*(rho - z_t), 0, mu_max)`, where `rho = B/T` is the target spend per round and `z_t` is this round's spend. Spending above target raises mu, which lowers the next bids. The guarantees are asymptotic: O(√T) regret for i.i.d. second-price auctions, and O(T^{3/4}) for adversarial or non-stationary settings. At T=68, √68 ≈ 8.2 and 68^{3/4} ≈ 23.7, so the bounds are loose compared with T. The useful parts at this horizon are the update's structure and its "never runs out too early" lemma, not the regret constant.

### Cited Findings
- **Algorithm (exact). [THEORY]** Gaitonde et al., Algorithm 1, attributed to Balseiro & Gur. Inputs are budget B_k, horizon T, step size ε_k > 0 and an upper bound µ̄. Initialize µ_{k,1}=0, B_{k,1}=B_k, ρ_k=B_k/T. Each round: observe v_{k,t}; bid `b_{k,t} = min(v_{k,t}/(1+µ_{k,t}), B_{k,t})`; observe spend z_{k,t}; set `B_{k,t+1} ← B_{k,t} − z_{k,t}` and `µ_{k,t+1} ← P_[0,µ̄](µ_{k,t} − ε_k(ρ_k − z_{k,t}))`; stop if B_{k,t} ≤ 0. The paper says "if ρ_k is above (resp., below) the current expenditure z_{k,t}, the agent decreases (resp., increases) her multiplier". — [Gaitonde, Li, Light, Lucier, Slivkins, "Budget Pacing in Repeated Auctions: Regret and Efficiency without Convergence" (arXiv 2205.08674 v6)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.08674)
- **Meaning of mu. [THEORY]** Google's autobidding survey derives the bid from LP duality. For a utility maximizer (λ=1) with only a budget constraint, the bid is `b_j = v_j/(1+α_B)`, where α_B is the optimal dual variable (shadow price) of the budget constraint. The survey attributes this formula to Balseiro et al. (2015). Read 1/(1+mu) as "one unit of cash is worth (1+mu) units of value right now". mu=0 means bid full value (truthful in a second-price auction). A large mu means cash is scarce, so shade hard. — [Aggarwal et al. (Google), "Auto-bidding and Auctions in Online Advertising: A Survey", SIGecom Exchanges 22(1), 2024](https://sigecom.org/exchanges/volume_22/1/AGGARWAL.pdf)
- **Same update in the survey's notation, with step size. [THEORY]** α_{B,0}=0; `α_{B,j+1} = max(α_{B,j} + η·(p_j − B/m), 0)` (sign arranged so overspend raises the price). η "is usually chosen to be of order η ≈ m^{-1/2}". "Budget constraints are usually enforced strictly and the algorithm stops bidding whenever the budget is depleted." The survey calls this "self-correcting": it keeps spend per auction near B/m. — [Aggarwal et al. survey](https://sigecom.org/exchanges/volume_22/1/AGGARWAL.pdf)
- **Stochastic guarantee. [THEORY]** Balseiro & Gur prove `sup_P E[OPT − ALG] = O(√m)` for i.i.d. (value, price) pairs, under restrictive distributional assumptions that Balseiro et al. (2023) later relaxed. — [Aggarwal et al. survey](https://sigecom.org/exchanges/volume_22/1/AGGARWAL.pdf); original: [Balseiro & Gur, Management Science 65(9):3952–3968, 2019](https://www.gsb.stanford.edu/faculty-research/publications/learning-repeated-auctions-budgets-regret-minimization-equilibrium)
- **Optimal policy is linear pacing, and the strategies form an approximate equilibrium. [THEORY]** Balseiro & Gur show matching lower and upper bounds, so the adaptive pacing class is asymptotically optimal against arbitrary competing bids. When all bidders use it, the dynamics converge to a tractable steady state. In a regime motivated by display advertising, these strategies form an approximate Nash equilibrium. — [Balseiro & Gur (Stanford GSB listing)](https://www.gsb.stanford.edu/faculty-research/publications/learning-repeated-auctions-budgets-regret-minimization-equilibrium)
- **Does not run out of budget too early. [THEORY]** Lemma 2.2 assumes values ≤ v̄, µ̄ ≥ v̄/ρ − 1 and ε·v̄ ≤ 1. Under these conditions the stopping time τ satisfies `T − τ ≤ µ̄/(ε ρ) + v̄/ρ` almost surely, for any sequence of values and competing prices. Remark 2.3: the algorithm never overbids (b ≤ v). — [Gaitonde et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.08674)
- **Non-stationary / adversarial guarantee. [THEORY]** Theorem 4.8 and Corollary 4.9 bound pacing regret by `O(√(T(P+1)/ε + εT))`, where P bounds the path length Σ|µ*_{t+1} − µ*_t| of the per-round ideal multiplier. With ε = 1/√T and P unknown, regret is `√(P+1)·O(T^{3/4})`. The authors call this "the first non-trivial regret bound for budget-constrained online bidding in the adversarial environment". If the ideal multiplier changes at most N times, take P = µ̄N. — [Gaitonde et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.08674)
- **Variants with the same guarantees. [THEORY]** PacingOGD (optimistic): `µ_{t+1} ← P(µ_t − ε∇̃_t − ε′∇̃_{t−1})` with ε′ ∈ [−ε/2, 0]. PacingOMD and PacingOFTRL use a gradient prediction M_t. Here ∇̃_t = ρ − z_t. — [Gaitonde et al., §5](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.08674)
- **Dual mirror descent (Balseiro, Lu, Mirrokni). [THEORY]** The method keeps one dual multiplier per resource and updates it by online mirror descent. The reference function sets the update: a Euclidean reference gives dual subgradient descent; an entropy reference gives dual exponential weights, i.e. multiplicative updates. The paper claims sublinear regret, of optimal order when horizon and budget scale proportionally. It applies the method to repeated auctions with budgets. — [Balseiro, Lu, Mirrokni, ICML 2020 (PMLR v119)](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v119/balseiro20a.html); journal version [Operations Research 71(1), 2023](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/opre.2021.2242)
- **DMD in Google's description. [PROD claim, no metrics]** The target is uniform resource consumption over time. Each step picks the action that maximizes "reward minus the opportunity cost of resources", then updates the dual by mirror descent on g_t = (uniform target − actual consumption). The method "attains performance close to optimal in both stochastic and adversarial input models while being oblivious to the structure of the input model". It "has been used over time at Google to help advertisers capture more value". No numbers are given. — [Google Research blog, "Robust Online Allocation with Dual Mirror Descent"](https://research.google/blog/robust-online-allocation-with-dual-mirror-descent/)
- **Exponentiated (multiplicative) form of the same update. [THEORY]** With the entropy reference h(µ)=Σµ log µ, mirror descent becomes `µ_{t+1} = µ_t ⊙ exp(−η g_t)`. — [Yang, Li, Islam, Ren, "Online Allocation with Replenishable Budgets" (arXiv 2401.04340)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.04340)
- **Practitioner form (DOGD) with λ = 1+mu. [GUIDE]** Bid `b_t = r_t/λ_t`. Update `λ_t ← λ_t − ε_t(B/T − 1{r_t>λ_t c_t}·c_t)`. Mini-batch form: `λ ← λ − ε(R(t)/T·B − S(t))`. Normalized form: `λ̃_{t+1} = λ̃_t − η_t(1 − ρ_t)`, where ρ_t = (S(t)/R(t))/(B/T) is the delivery ratio and λ̃ = λ/λ_norm with λ_norm ≈ λ_init. On normalization: "choosing it too small can slow convergence, whereas choosing it too large can amplify noise and produce oscillatory updates". — [Y. Chen, "A Practical Guide to Budget Pacing Algorithms in Digital Advertising" (arXiv 2503.06942 v2), ch. 5](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.06942)

### Inferences
- In this game, `v_t` is the bot's estimated marginal point value of the tile, expressed in cash units. `mu` is the current exchange rate between cash and points. At the rival price level (~8.7) and a typical tile value v, a well-paced mu satisfies v/(1+mu) ≈ the clearing price we want to beat. For example, v=13 gives 1+mu ≈ 1.5.
- With T=68, the textbook step size ε ≈ 1/√T ≈ 0.12 per unit of normalized error (spend/ρ − 1) is a reasonable start. Because a single tile purchase (~9) is many times ρ (~1.5–3 per round), the raw per-round gradient is very lumpy. Use a normalized or cumulative error (see Q6).
- The regret bounds do not by themselves justify the method at T=68. The case rests on the structural optimality of linear pacing (Balseiro & Gur), robustness to arbitrary competitor bids, and the early-depletion bound. Plugging in plausible numbers (µ̄≈2, ε≈0.12, ρ≈1.5, v̄≈20) gives T−τ ≲ 11 + 13. That bound is loose, but the qualitative guarantee holds.

### Gaps
- I could not open the Balseiro & Gur paper itself (paywalled). The exact constants of their step-size rule and Theorem 3.3 come only through the Gaitonde and Aggarwal summaries.
- The explicit O(√T) constant and the adversarial competitive ratio of dual mirror descent could not be read: the PMLR abstract page omits them, and the arXiv PDF URL returned 404.
- I found no paper that evaluates these algorithms at horizons as short as ~68 auctions. All analyses are asymptotic, and simulations use thousands to millions of auctions.

## Q2. Conitzer et al. pacing equilibria (multiplicative, first-price), and what happens when several bidders pace at once

### Takeaway
When every bidder uses a multiplicative pacing multiplier α ∈ [0,1] (bid = α·v), a second-price pacing equilibrium (SPPE) always exists. It can be non-unique, and approximating one is PPAD-complete. A first-price pacing equilibrium (FPPE) is unique and computable via the Eisenberg–Gale convex program. When all bidders run gradient pacing at the same time, the dynamics need not converge, yet aggregate liquid welfare stays at least half of optimal. Microsoft's Bing-based simulations show vanishing individual regret empirically.

### Cited Findings
- **SPPE. [THEORY]** Each buyer scales all bids by a multiplier in [0,1]. "Pacing equilibria always exist". Markets may have multiple equilibria with widely different objective values, but multiplicity was "very rare" on random instance families. Computing a welfare- or revenue-maximizing equilibrium is NP-hard (the authors give a MIP). Static MIP solutions can beat a dynamic pacing algorithm on instances based on a real auction market. With enough participants, buyers had no incentive to misreport. — [Conitzer, Kroer, Sodomka, Stier-Moses, "Multiplicative Pacing Equilibria in Auction Markets" (arXiv 1706.07151; Operations Research 2022)](https://arxiv.org/abs/1706.07151)
- **Why multiplicative pacing rather than throttling.** Multiplicative pacing lets a bidder participate in more auctions and win at lower prices than probabilistic throttling. Conitzer et al. cite Balseiro et al. as concluding that multiplicative pacing is buyer-optimal among the strategies studied. (This is a secondary report of Balseiro et al.'s finding; see Q3.) — [Conitzer et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1706.07151)
- **Complexity. [THEORY]** Finding an approximate SPPE is PPAD-complete (Chen, Kroer, Kumar). — [Chen, Kroer, Kumar, "The Complexity of Pacing for Second-Price Auctions" (arXiv 2103.13969)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.13969)
- **FPPE. [THEORY + SIM]** First-price auctions "guarantee uniqueness of the steady-state equilibrium of the budget management system", along with monotonicity. The equilibrium is computable via the Eisenberg–Gale convex program. Bidders have small regret relative to their best ex-post strategy, and budget-constrained bidders tend to have smaller regret. — [Conitzer, Kroer, Panigrahi et al., "Pacing Equilibrium in First Price Auction Markets" (arXiv 1811.07166; Management Science 68(12), 2022)](https://arxiv.org/abs/1811.07166)
- **Throttling equilibria. [THEORY]** A throttling equilibrium always exists in second-price auctions. It may not be unique, and for some games every one is irrational. Finding a revenue-maximizing approximate throttling equilibrium is NP-hard. In first-price auctions, the revenue of the unique throttling equilibrium and that of the unique pacing equilibrium are within a factor of 2 of each other. — [Chen, Kroer, Kumar, "Throttling Equilibria in Auction Markets" (arXiv 2107.10923)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2107.10923)
- **Simultaneous pacing without convergence. [THEORY]** If every agent runs Algorithm 1, possibly with different step sizes, then `W(x,F) ≥ W(y,F)/2 − O(n v̄ √(T log(v̄nT)))` for any core auction (first-price, second-price, GSP). This "holds without requiring convergence of the dynamics" and matches the best guarantee available even for a pure Nash equilibrium of a static truthful auction. — [Gaitonde et al., Thm 3.1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.08674)
- **Plain no-regret is not enough. [THEORY]** "A natural no-regret condition alone cannot suffice for liquid welfare" (counterexample in their App. B.2). — [Gaitonde et al., §5](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.08674)
- **Multi-agent simulation on Bing data. [SIM]** About 2.4M auctions over 7 days (April 2022), with every campaign that has ≥1000 auctions bidding via the algorithm at once. Multipliers were warm-started proportional to 1/ρ_k, and ε ∝ 1/√T_k was tuned by grid search. Result: "simultaneous execution of Algorithm 1 yields vanishing regret, with regret rate O(T^α) for α ≤ 3/5". The multiplicative-update method (MU, scale µ by (1±ε) on over/under spend) "is dominated by Algorithm 1 on both liquid welfare and regret rate". Optimistic GD performed comparably to Algorithm 1. — [Gaitonde et al., §6](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.08674)
- **Budget plus ROI autobidders together. [THEORY]** A gradient-based algorithm using only bandit feedback satisfies both budget and ROI constraints and gets vanishing individual regret, even adversarially. When all autobidders use it, liquid welfare is at least half of optimal, again without needing convergence. It reduces to budget pacing when only the budget constraint is present. — [Lucier, Pattathil, Slivkins, Zhang, "Autobidders with Budget and ROI Constraints" (COLT 2024; arXiv 2301.13306)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.13306)
- **Price of anarchy. [THEORY]** For uniform-bidding value maximizers in second-price auctions, PoA ≤ 2 and the bound is tight (2 − ε instances exist, even with 2 bidders). FPA also has PoA_PNE = 2, while PoA_NE(FPA) ≈ 2.188. — [Aggarwal et al. survey, §4.3](https://sigecom.org/exchanges/volume_22/1/AGGARWAL.pdf)
- **Uniform bid scaling is optimal in truthful auctions. [THEORY]** For value maximizers, uniform bid scaling (bid κ·v with one universal factor) is optimal in auctions that are truthful for quasi-linear bidders. In FPA/GSP, non-uniform scaling can do much better. With uniform scaling only, synthetic experiments rank FPA > GSP > VCG on welfare and profit. — [Aggarwal et al. survey, §6.3](https://sigecom.org/exchanges/volume_22/1/AGGARWAL.pdf)

### Inferences
- The game is second-price with 6 players. If several rival bots also pace, an equilibrium exists but may be non-unique. Our bot should not assume a stable price level; it should keep adapting mu. The rivals' observed 7.8–9.6 per-tile price band is consistent with a population of paced bidders who shade below value.
- A bot that bids near full value early, as ours appears to, raises the second-price clearing price only when it is the second-highest bidder. Mostly it wins tiles and pays the second-highest rival bid. Its average price of 13.3 against the rivals' ~8.7 suggests it often wins auctions where another bidder bid high, i.e. contested tiles, which pacing would let it skip.

### Gaps
- No paper studies pacing equilibria with only ~6 bidders and ~68 items. Equilibrium results assume either many auctions or continuous values.
- I could not confirm the exact dominance ranking (throttling vs. multiplicative boosting, buyer utility vs. seller revenue) in Balseiro, Kim, Mahdian, Mirrokni from the primary text; only a secondary citation was available.

## Q3. What production systems use (PID, throttling vs. bid modification, ORTB, RL; Google/Meta/Alibaba/Yahoo/LinkedIn/Snap/DoorDash) and measured gains

### Takeaway
Production pacing falls into two families. Bid modification (multiplicative bid shading via PID or dual gradient) is the theory-preferred form. Throttling (probabilistic participation) is the robust legacy form used at LinkedIn and Yahoo. The strongest quantified live gains: Yahoo smart pacing cut eCPC 8.9–79% while holding delivery error at 5–14%; RL bidding on Vlion lifted clicks 44.7% vs a linear bidder; Snap's damped multiplicative controller cut pacing error 13% and λ-volatility 54%; DoorDash's end-of-day finish cut overdelivery from 7.0% to 4.7%. Alibaba's multivariable PID reached a value ratio of 0.928 vs 0.549 for the classic feedback controller, but only in offline replay.

### Cited Findings
- **Throttling vs. bid modification. [GUIDE/survey]** Throttling sets a pacing rate, the probability of entering an auction. Bid modification adjusts the bid. Bid modification "depends on predicting market competition", while throttling "normally work[s] well in dynamic marketplaces". Feedback-control bid modification in RTB includes Chen et al. (2011), Karlsson & Zhang (2013) and Zhang et al. (2016). — [Wang, Zhang, Yuan, "Display Advertising with Real-Time Bidding (RTB) and Behavioural Targeting" (arXiv 1610.03013)](https://arxiv.org/abs/1610.03013)
- **Six strategies compared at system equilibrium. [THEORY + data]** The strategies are probabilistic throttling, thresholding, bid shading, reserve pricing, and two forms of multiplicative boosting. A system equilibrium exists, and dominance relations hold in a simplified setting, "confirmed using real ad auction data from sponsored search". — [Balseiro, Kim, Mahdian, Mirrokni, "Budget-Management Strategies in Repeated Auctions", Operations Research 69(3), 2021 (Google Research)](https://research.google/pubs/budget-management-strategies-in-repeated-auctions/)
- **Throttling update rule. [GUIDE]** `p(t) = min{p(t−1)(1+λ_t), 1}` if S(t) ≤ α(t) = (t/T)·B, else `max{p(t−1)(1−λ_t), 0}`. — [Chen, Practical Guide, ch. 2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.06942)
- **PID pacing. [GUIDE]** Target spend per bucket is `TS(t) = NR(t)/ΣNR(s) · B`, proportional to forecast supply. With error e = s(t_k) − r(t_k), the controller is `u = Kp·e + Ki·Σe·Δt + Kd·Δe/Δt`, and the actuator is `b ← b·exp(u)`. Shared gains across campaigns require a normalized error `e = 1 − r/s`. A PID "cannot correct a target that is constructed from supply volume alone"; the target must reflect predicted value and cost too. — [Chen, Practical Guide, ch. 3](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.06942)
- **Zhang et al., feedback control of RTB. [PROD, qualitative]** A PID-style controller adjusts bids to hold the KPI (win rate or eCPC) at a reference. Holding eCPC at the optimal reference maximizes clicks under a budget. The paper reports deployment on a commercial RTB platform, where an online test showed "controllable" performance. The controller form, from the survey: `φ = λ_P e + λ_I Σe Δt + λ_D Δe/Δt; b_a = b·exp(φ)`. — [Zhang, Rong, Wang, Zhu, Wang, WSDM 2016 (arXiv 1603.01055)](https://arxiv.org/abs/1603.01055); [survey eq. 5.60–5.62](https://arxiv.org/abs/1610.03013)
- **Alibaba multivariable control. [SIM, offline replay on Taobao logs]** The optimal bid from the primal-dual LP is `bid_i = (1/(p+q))·CTR_i·CVR_i + (q/(p+q))·CTR_i·C`, with p the budget dual and q the CPC dual. The PID error drives an actuator `x(t+1) = x(0)·exp(−u(t))` with hourly steps (T=24). Gains were grid-searched (values not reported). Setup: 40 campaigns, ~20M bid logs. Value ratio at CPC ratio 1.0: Cost-min 0.362, Fb-Control 0.549, Fb-Control-M 0.709, I-PID 0.892, M-PID 0.928. No online A/B is reported. — [Yang et al. (Alibaba), "Bid Optimization by Multivariable Control in Display Advertising", KDD 2019 (arXiv 1905.10928)](https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.10928)
- **ORTB (Zhang, Yuan, Wang). [THEORY]** With win function `w(b) = b/(b+l)`, the optimal first-price bid is `b(r) = √(u(r)·l/λ + l²) − l`, concave in value. The second-price optimal bid is linear, `b = u(r)/λ`; with uniform market prices, λ = ½·∛(T/(B l²)). λ is the budget shadow price, so the second-price ORTB bid is the same linear pacing form as v/(1+mu). — [Wang/Zhang/Yuan survey, eqs. 5.19–5.38](https://arxiv.org/abs/1610.03013); [Zhang, Yuan, Wang, KDD 2014 (DBLP)](https://dblp.uni-trier.de/rec/conf/kdd/ZhangYW14.html)
- **RL bidding (Cai et al.). [SIM + PROD]** MDP state (t remaining auctions, b remaining budget). The value function `V(t,b) = max_a Σ_δ≤a m(δ)(θ + V(t−1,b−δ)) + Σ_δ>a m(δ)V(t−1,b)` is solved by dynamic programming with a non-parametric market-price distribution m(δ). The bid is the largest a with `g(a) = θ + V(t−1,b−a) − V(t−1,b) ≥ 0`. A neural net fits D(t,b) = V(t,b+1) − V(t,b). Offline iPinYou at T=1000: +22.39% clicks at a 1/32 budget, +6.58% at 1/2, beating linear in 46/50 settings; YOYI +7.4% at 1/8. Online A/B on the Vlion DSP over 5 campaigns, 25–28 July 2016: "44.7% improvement in click performance" vs a linear bidder, with lower eCPC/CPM and more even spend. — [Cai et al., "Real-Time Bidding by Reinforcement Learning in Display Advertising", WSDM 2017 (arXiv 1701.02490)](https://arxiv.org/abs/1701.02490)
- **Yahoo Smart Pacing. [PROD + SIM]** Throttling by layers grouped by predicted response rate, with higher layers paced first. If the residual is positive, rates rise top-down (cap 1). If negative, rates fall bottom-up (floor 0). Each adjacent layer gets a small "trial rate" for exploration. Cold start uses r_G=0.01, L=⌈1/r_G⌉ layers and a 1% trial budget. Live A/B on 3 campaigns: eCPC −72%, −67%, −79% vs baseline, with similar per-slot spend error (6.4–10.2%). 2-week test: eCPC −8.9% to −49.2% across 4 campaigns. Offline, per-slot error was 18% vs 96% for the LinkedIn method, with ~70% lower eCPC. — [Xu, Lee, Li, Qi, Lu (Yahoo), "Smart Pacing for Effective Online Ad Campaign Optimization", KDD 2015 (arXiv 1506.05851)](https://arxiv.org/abs/1506.05851)
- **LinkedIn. [PROD, numbers not retrieved]** A pacing component that probabilistically throttles fast-spending campaigns using a pass-through rate, built on global supply patterns. Benefits are shown in LinkedIn experiments. — [Agarwal, Ghosh, Wei, You, "Budget Pacing for Targeted Online Advertisements at LinkedIn", KDD 2014](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/2623330.2623366)
- **Snap small-budget pacing. [PROD, live traffic split]** Baseline: multiplicative `λ_{t+1} = λ_t(1 ± α)` with adaptive α, plus a tolerance band ε of no update. Bucketized hysteresis controller (BHC): error `E = (θ_d − θ_o)/θ_d`, band k chosen by |E|, update `λ_t = λ_{t−1}(1 + s_k·sgn(E))`. Metrics are pacing error PE = mean |actual − target|/target and λ-volatility CV_λ = std(λ)/mean(λ). Results vs production: BHC PE +88%, volatility +133% (worse); AOF +173%, +210% (worst); ALU PE +70%, volatility −2.6%; the damped SSDM variant PE −13.06%, volatility −53.78%, CPM −1.07% (best). — [Apparaju, Niu, Qi (Snap), "Feedback Control for Small Budget Pacing" (arXiv 2509.25429)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.25429)
- **DoorDash Smart Fast Finish. [PROD]** One-week budget-split A/B: average live hours +45 min (+4.2%); soft overdelivery 7.0% → 4.7%; for the largest 28 campaigns, 7.7% → 4.1%. Offline, full SFF gave +8% or more live hours vs traditional fast finish. — [DoorDash, "Smart Fast Finish: Preventing Overdelivery via Daily Budget Pacing" (arXiv 2509.07929)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.07929)
- **Karlsson (AOL/Verizon Media/Amazon). [GUIDE]** Feedback control is described as "critical in the scalable optimization of Internet advertising". Karlsson led the campaign-control research group at Advertising.com/AOL, and his later work covers multivariable feedback control for multi-constraint campaigns. — [Yahoo Research listing, "Feedback Control in Programmatic Advertising", IEEE Control Systems Magazine 2020](https://www.yahooinc.com/research/publications/feedback-control-in-programmatic-advertising-the-frontier-of-optimization-in-real-time-bidding); [Karlsson, "Multivariable feedback control for multi-constraint optimization in online advertising" (Amazon Science PDF)](https://assets.amazon.science/eb/ef/cfc728a845dc945ccf7f619ff534/scipub-approval152129-37032870-multivariable-feedback-control-for-multiconstraint-optimization-in-online-advertising.pdf)
- **MPC alternative. [SIM]** Online isotonic regression builds monotone bid→spend and bid→result curves, with a one-step receding-horizon controller. The paper claims significantly better spend efficiency and cost control than baselines, in simulation only. — [Chen, Zhu, Xia, Wang, "A Lightweight MPC Bidding Framework for Brand Auction Ads" (arXiv 2603.07721)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.07721)

### Inferences
- For a 68-round, single-agent bot, pure throttling wastes information: we can compute a per-tile value and bid on every tile. Bid modification (v/(1+mu)) matches the theory and the "buyer-optimal" result. Throttling-style ideas are still useful as a hard gate: skip tiles whose value is below the shaded clearing price.
- Cai et al.'s DP is directly feasible here. T=68 and cash ≤ a few hundred units gives a ~68×300 table, trivially computable each second if m(δ) (the clearing-price distribution) is estimated from logs. It is the "exact" alternative to linear pacing at short horizons. Yahoo's layered approach is equivalent to bidding only on high-value tile categories when behind budget.

### Gaps

## Q4. Estimating win-rate and market-price landscapes from censored data (survival models; Wu et al.; Wang/Zhang; DLF)

### Takeaway
In RTB, losing bids reveal only that the market price was at least the bid (right-censoring). The standard fix is the Kaplan–Meier product-limit estimator `w(b) = 1 − Π_{b_j<b}(n_j − d_j)/n_j`, or censored regression and deep survival models. Our game's feedback differs: it shows the winning bids of all auctions after each match. Our own losing bids are not shown, but those are known to us, so censoring is much milder than in RTB.

### Cited Findings
- **Censoring and the naive estimator's bias. [THEORY/example]** "it only knows that the winning price is higher than the bid" when the DSP loses. The observation-only estimator `w_o(b) = #{z<b}/|D|` "overestimates the winning probability" (worked example: w=2/7 vs w_o=2/4 at b=2). The Kaplan–Meier estimator: losing probability `l(b) = Π_{b_j<b}(n_j−d_j)/n_j`, `w(b) = 1 − l(b)`, with d_j wins at price b_j−1 and n_j at-risk auctions. — [Wang/Zhang/Yuan survey §3.3 (eqs. 3.18–3.20)](https://arxiv.org/abs/1610.03013)
- **Wu, Yeh, Chen (KDD 2015). [SIM]** Borrows censored regression (Tobit-style, from survival analysis and econometrics) so that losing data enters the loss. Since real data violates the regression assumptions, a mixture model weights linear and censored regression by predicted win rate. "Censored regression usually outperforms linear regression, and the averaged model always outperforms linear regression." — [Wu, NTU dissertation (2019) and KDD 2015 work](https://tdr.lib.ntu.edu.tw/handle/123456789/7399); also summarized in [Zhang et al. survey (arXiv 2210.07770)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.07770)
- **Deep Landscape Forecasting (Ren et al., KDD 2019). [SIM]** An RNN predicts the conditional winning probability at each price; the chain rule gives the full distribution. The loss combines won (uncensored) and lost (censored) auctions, with no distributional shape assumed. It beats prior methods on iPinYou and YOYI. — [Ren et al., "Deep Landscape Forecasting for Real-time Bidding Advertising" (arXiv 1905.03028)](https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.03028)
- **Bid-aware gradient descent (Zhang, Zhou, Wang, Xu; KDD 2016). [PROD]** CTR/bid models trained only on won auctions are biased. Reweighting each training example by 1/w(b) (KM win probability), `θ ← (1−ηλ)θ + (η/w)(y − σ(θᵀx))x`, removes the bias. Online A/B on Yahoo's RTB platform: +2.97% AUC for CTR, −9.30% eCPC for bid optimization. — [Zhang et al., UCL Discovery](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/1503913/); update rule from [survey eq. 4.46](https://arxiv.org/abs/1610.03013)
- **Market-price distribution inside RL bidding.** Cai et al. use a non-parametric m(δ) estimate, optionally per segment m_i(δ), as the DP transition model. — [Cai et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1701.02490)
- **Cold-start initialization from logs. [GUIDE]** Replay historical auctions to find b_init whose expected spend per opportunity matches B/T, then set λ_init = 1/b_init. Without history, run "a bounded cold-start exploration procedure" over a small bid range. — [Chen, Practical Guide §5.1.2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.06942)

### Inferences
- **Our feedback is closer to uncensored than RTB's.** The second-price "market price" relevant to us is d_t = the highest rival bid. Two cases depend on what "winning bid" means in the logs. (a) If it is the winner's bid, then in every auction we lost, the logged winning bid equals d_t exactly, with no censoring. In auctions we won, our price paid equals d_t. So the full d_t series is observable post-match. (b) If it is the clearing price (second-highest bid overall), then in auctions we lost where our bid was the second-highest, the price equals our own bid. That gives only a lower bound on d_t (left-censored at our bid). Kaplan–Meier can be applied with censoring reversed. The team should check which field the logs contain.
- **Practical estimator for 68 rounds × N past matches.** Pool d_t by game phase (early/mid/late, e.g. thirds) and by tile letter class (vowel, common consonant, rare high-score). Fit an empirical CDF per cell, reweighted toward recent matches. That gives F(price | phase, class), enough to compute the expected cost and win probability of any bid: `win(b) = F(b)`, `E[pay | win] = E[d | d<b]`.
- Because rivals' clearing prices (7.8–9.6 average) are visible, a strong prior is mu_init such that typical v/(1+mu_init) ≈ 9.

### Gaps
- I did not retrieve quantitative accuracy tables for Wu et al. (2015) or DLF (only qualitative "outperforms" claims).
- No source covers landscape estimation with only tens of observations per match. Pooling across matches is our own inference.

## Q5. Non-stationary value, end-of-horizon spend-down, mid-game income; replenishing budgets and return-on-spend (Aggarwal–Badanidiyuru–Mehta value maximizers)

### Takeaway
The literature points to three changes to plain pacing. (i) Price conservatively on income: compute the reference spend rate from cash in hand, not from forecast income, and use income as it arrives (OACP). (ii) Use a time-varying target schedule, re-planned each round from remaining cash and rounds, to handle non-stationarity and horizon uncertainty (Balseiro–Kroer–Kumar; path-length regret). (iii) Run a controlled spend-down near the end with a smooth transition, not an abrupt switch (DoorDash SFF). If leftover cash has no value at match end, the value-maximizer framing (bid ∝ value under a return-on-spend or budget constraint) is the right objective, because utility maximization undervalues spending cash that is otherwise worthless.

### Cited Findings
- **Replenishable budgets, OACP. [THEORY + SIM]** Each round: receive f_t and potential replenishment Ê_t; actual `E_t = min(Ê_t, B_max − B_t)`. Pre-select `x̂_t = argmax f_t(x) − µ_tᵀx`. If x̂_t ≤ B_t + E_t, act and set `g_t = −x̂_t + ρ`; else x_t = 0, g_t = 0. Then `B_{t+1} = B_t + E_t − x_t` and a mirror-descent dual step. The key design choice is that the reference per-round budget stays ρ = B_1/T "as if no budget replenishment were received", because "aggressively using ρ̃ = ρ + E_t as the reference … can result in an unnecessarily low resource price". Replenishment is still used "opportunistically" through the available budget B_t + E_t. OACP's asymptotic competitive ratio is optimal without further assumptions on replenishment; OACP+ improves it if replenishment arrives at least every T* rounds. Validated in simulation (renewable-powered AI inference, not ads). — [Yang, Li, Islam, Ren, "Online Allocation with Replenishable Budgets: Worst Case and Beyond", Proc. ACM Meas. Anal. Comput. Syst. 8(1), 2024 (arXiv 2401.04340)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.04340)
- **Bandits with replenishable knapsacks. [THEORY]** A primal-dual template for resources whose consumption can be negative (replenishment). Gives a constant competitive ratio for adversarial inputs when B = Ω(T) or per-round replenishment is a positive constant, and Õ(T^{1/2}) regret for stochastic inputs. It extends Balseiro et al.'s best-of-both-worlds approach, which assumed monotone consumption. — [Bernasconi, Castiglioni, Celli, Fusco, ICLR 2024 (arXiv 2306.08470)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.08470)
- **Horizon uncertainty and time-varying targets. [THEORY]** If the horizon is only known to lie in [τ1, τ2], no algorithm can achieve more than about 1/ln(τ2/τ1) of the hindsight optimum. The proposed fix is dual mirror descent with "a schedule of time-varying target consumption rates", plus a fast method to compute a near-optimal schedule and to incorporate horizon predictions. — [Balseiro, Kroer, Kumar, "Online Resource Allocation under Horizon Uncertainty" (arXiv 2206.13606)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.13606)
- **Non-stationary values. [THEORY]** Theorem 4.8 and Extension 4.11 hold when value distributions and the auction change arbitrarily between rounds. Regret scales with √(P+1), where P is the path length of the ideal multiplier. — [Gaitonde et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.08674)
- **Non-i.i.d. supply breaks proportional targets. [GUIDE]** Two intervals with equal request counts can have very different value and cost. The target should "incorporate the predicted quality and cost of future supply", or use an algorithm designed for time-varying inputs. If remaining supply is overestimated the controller under-spends; if underestimated it "may spend too aggressively at the beginning". — [Chen, Practical Guide §3.2.3](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.06942)
- **End-of-horizon spend-down (Fast Finish). [PROD]** Standard practice switches at end of day to "spend remaining budget as fast as possible". DoorDash's SFF instead starts the finish at a campaign-specific time: `t = D·√(OR − min_OR) + min_start`, with min_start = 0.85 and max_start = 0.95 of the day, min_OR = 1.03 and max_OR = 1.50. It then lowers throttling linearly over a 1-hour transition window rather than dropping instantly. A ratchet lets the start time only move later, to avoid a feedback loop. — [DoorDash SFF (arXiv 2509.07929)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.07929)
- **Value maximizers with budget and return-on-spend constraints. [THEORY]** With duals α_T (RoS, target τ) and α_B (budget), the optimal value-maximizer bid in a truthful auction is `b_j = ((1 + τ·α_T)/(α_T + α_B))·v_j`. With only a RoS constraint, `b_j = (1/α_T + τ)·v_j`. With a budget-constrained utility maximizer, `b_j = v_j/(1+α_B)`. Feng et al. (2023) give a dual-based value-maximizer algorithm with regret O(√m) and RoS violation O(√m log m), plus a strictly feasible variant. — [Aggarwal et al. survey, eqs. 6–8 and §3.2](https://sigecom.org/exchanges/volume_22/1/AGGARWAL.pdf)
- **Uniform bidding optimal for value maximizers (ABM 2019). [THEORY]** For value maximizers in truthful auctions, a single bid-scaling factor κ (bid κ·v) is optimal. — [Aggarwal et al. survey §6.3, citing Aggarwal, Badanidiyuru, Mehta, WINE 2019](https://sigecom.org/exchanges/volume_22/1/AGGARWAL.pdf)
- **ROI plus budget pacing jointly. [THEORY]** — [Lucier et al. (arXiv 2301.13306)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.13306)

### Inferences (including recommended pacing rule)
- **Spend scale.** If rivals buy ~25 tiles at ~8.7, their match spend is roughly 220, more than double the starting 100. Income therefore funds over half of a top bot's purchases. Fully conservative OACP pricing (reference ρ = 100/68 ≈ 1.47, ignoring income) would make our bot under-spend. The literature's principle (do not price as if forecast income is guaranteed) should be applied partially: include a discounted forecast of income in the target.
- **Recompute the target each round.** Use a receding-horizon target from cash in hand plus discounted expected income over remaining rounds, rather than the fixed ρ = B/T. This follows Balseiro–Kroer–Kumar's time-varying schedules and the Practical Guide's advice to re-forecast remaining supply.
- **Value objective.** Leftover cash presumably earns nothing at match end (verify from game rules). If so, the bot is a value maximizer with a budget, and the multiplier should be pushed toward 0 (bid ≈ v) as the end nears and cash remains. But tile value v itself falls near the end, since there is less time to form words. That decay must enter v_t; the pacer must never bid above v_t just to spend down.
- **Smooth the finish, ratchet the trigger.** Following SFF, ramp mu down over a window (e.g. the last ~10–15 rounds) rather than switching at a fixed round, and avoid triggers that feed back on themselves.

Recommended rule (adaptive dual pacing with conservative income, cumulative-error update, smooth spend-down). This is our synthesis, not from any single paper:

```text
CONSTANTS (tune via simulator; start values shown)
  T          = 68           # expected number of auctions; re-estimate live
  ETA        = 0.15         # step on normalized error (≈1/sqrt(T) ≈ 0.12, round up)
  MU_MAX     = 3.0          # bid ≥ v/4; satisfies Balseiro-Gur µ̄ ≥ v̄/ρ − 1 only loosely
  MU_MIN     = 0.0          # never bid above value (Gaitonde Remark 2.3)
  MAX_STEP   = 0.25         # cap |Δmu| per round (Practical Guide ±10%/update analogue)
  DEADBAND   = 0.10         # no update if |cumulative error| < 10% (Snap tolerance band)
  KAPPA      = 0.6          # fraction of forecast income treated as spendable (OACP → 0, naive → 1)
  FINISH_LEN = 12           # rounds of smooth spend-down (SFF transition window analogue)
  RESERVE    = 0            # keep 0 unless leftover cash has terminal value

STATE
  mu    = mu_init           # warm start: median(v_hist) / median(clearing_price_hist) − 1, clipped
  spent = 0                 # cumulative spend this match
  plan  = 0                 # cumulative target spend this match

EACH AUCTION t (1..T), tile with estimated marginal value v_t (points → cash units):
  cash      = current cash (already includes income received so far)   # opportunistic use (OACP)
  rounds_left = max(1, T_est − t + 1)
  inc_fore  = forecast_income_remaining(t)                             # from own word plan / history
  rho_t     = (cash + KAPPA * inc_fore − RESERVE) / rounds_left        # receding-horizon target rate

  # end-of-horizon: shrink the price of cash smoothly toward 0 (value maximizer, cash worthless at end)
  if rounds_left <= FINISH_LEN:
      mu_eff = mu * (rounds_left / FINISH_LEN)
  else:
      mu_eff = mu

  bid = min(v_t / (1 + mu_eff), cash − RESERVE)
  bid = max(bid, 0)
  if v_t <= 0 or bid < min_useful_bid: bid = 0                          # throttle-gate low-value tiles

  submit(bid); observe price_paid z_t (0 if lost)

  # cumulative (integral-like) normalized error: robust to lumpy one-tile spends
  spent += z_t
  plan  += rho_t
  err    = (spent − plan) / max(plan, rho_t)                            # >0 overspending, <0 under
  if |err| > DEADBAND:
      delta = clip(ETA * err, −MAX_STEP, +MAX_STEP)
      mu    = clip(mu + delta, MU_MIN, MU_MAX)                          # overspend → raise price of cash

  record (t, phase, tile_class, observed clearing/winning price) for landscape estimate (Q4)

AFTER MATCH
  refit F(price | phase, tile_class) from all logged winning prices
  refit mu_init, KAPPA, inc_fore model; grid-search ETA, MU_MAX, FINISH_LEN in simulator
```

- This rule is Balseiro–Gur dual descent, `mu ← mu + ε(z − ρ)`, with five engineering changes, each tied to a cited failure mode. (1) The error is normalized and cumulative, because one tile purchase is many times ρ_t; the per-round SGD gradient would whipsaw mu (Snap's small-budget high-gain problem). (2) A deadband and (3) a per-step clamp reduce oscillation (Snap; Practical Guide §4.2). (4) The target is receding-horizon, with partial income credit (OACP vs. naive). (5) A smooth spend-down window is applied to mu (SFF).
- **Optional upgrade.** At this size, the Cai et al. DP over (t, cash) with the empirical price distribution F gives the exact optimal threshold bid each round. Linear pacing is its first-order approximation. It is worth testing in the simulator as the "ceiling" strategy.
- **Expected effect on the observed failure.** The current bot front-loads purchases (80% in the first half) at 13.3. Under this rule, early overspend drives err > 0, mu rises, and bids fall to v/(1+mu). The bot then wins only tiles whose value exceeds the shaded threshold, which should push its average price toward the rivals' 8–10 band and preserve cash for the late game. This is an untested hypothesis until simulated.

### Gaps
- No paper studies pacing where income is endogenous, i.e. replenishment caused by the bidder's own purchases (tiles → words → income). OACP and BwRK assume exogenous or adversarial replenishment. The value of a tile should arguably include the income it unlocks, which links the bid value to the budget. No source addresses this coupling.
- No source reports production results for replenishable-budget bidding in ads (ad budgets are fixed per day or flight). Replenishment evidence is simulation only, and in non-ad domains.
- Parameter values in the pseudocode (ETA, MU_MAX, KAPPA, FINISH_LEN, DEADBAND) are inferred starting points, not literature values. Gaitonde et al. tuned ε ∝ 1/√T by grid search and did not publish the coefficients.

## Q6. Documented failure modes (oscillation, budget flooring, under-delivery, cold start) and how they are prevented

### Takeaway
The documented failures are: oscillation or limit cycles from high loop gain or delayed feedback; lag and staircase spending from over-smoothing; early depletion, where the budget runs out before the end and blacks out late opportunities; under-delivery with an end-of-period rush; sensitivity to the cold-start bid; and overdelivery from delayed charges. Remedies with evidence: normalized error and damped multiplicative steps (Snap: −13% pacing error, −54% volatility, live); bounded per-update bid change; warm-start from replayed logs; time-varying targets; smooth end-of-period finish (DoorDash, live). The dual-gradient algorithm also has a proven bound on how early it can deplete.

### Cited Findings
- **Overspend early, blackout late.** "bidding too high early can leave advertisers with no budget, and thus missed opportunities later". — [Google Research blog on DMD](https://research.google/blog/robust-online-allocation-with-dual-mirror-descent/)
- **Bound on early depletion. [THEORY]** `T − τ ≤ µ̄/(ερ) + v̄/ρ`, under the conditions in Q1. — [Gaitonde et al., Lemma 2.2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.08674)
- **Oscillation from delayed feedback. [GUIDE]** With delayed spend signals, the controller "may interpret the temporarily low observed spend as under-delivery and continue increasing the bid, which can lead to oscillation or overspending after the delayed clicks arrive". Remedies: longer update interval, smaller gains, or estimating pending spend. — [Chen, Practical Guide §3.2.3](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.06942)
- **Oscillation from normalization or gain choice. [GUIDE]** A λ_norm that is too large "can amplify noise and produce oscillatory updates"; one too small slows convergence. — [Chen, Practical Guide §5.1.2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.06942)
- **Bid stability clamps. [GUIDE]** "a sudden drop in bids may result in under-delivery, while a sharp increase in bids could cause rapid budget depletion". Remedy: bound each new bid to `[0.9·b_t, 1.1·b_t]`, scaled to the update frequency. — [Chen, Practical Guide §4.2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.06942)
- **Small-budget pacing failures. [PROD]** Small budgets with large targeting groups raise open-loop gain, so minor λ changes cause large spend changes. A bang-bang-style bucketized controller produced "persistent oscillations in λ" (limit cycle, overshoot). Averaging feedback "introduced excessive lag" with staircase spending. Over-damped ramping took nearly half a day to converge, with severe underspending. The damped SSDM variant won: PE −13.06%, CV_λ −53.78%. — [Snap (arXiv 2509.25429)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.25429)
- **Under-delivery and end-of-period rush.** A small amount of under-delivery is common and "won't be contrasted fast enough, imposing a money rush toward the end of the campaign". — [arXiv 1808.03147, "A New Optimization Layer for Real-Time Bidding Advertising Campaigns" (search snippet; full text not read)](https://arxiv.org/abs/1808.03147)
- **Cold start. [GUIDE/SIM, from search snippet]** PID and DOGD degrade as the cold-start bid moves away from the true optimum. The initial bid is estimated offline, and performance can be highly sensitive to it. PID-style controllers tend to deplete the budget before the end of the day. — [Chen et al., MPC framework (arXiv 2603.07721)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.07721). Remedy: replay-based λ_init or bounded exploration. — [Practical Guide §5.1.2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.06942). Gaitonde et al. warm-start multipliers proportional to 1/ρ_k. — [Gaitonde et al. §6.2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.08674). Yahoo starts at a global rate r_G=0.01 with a 1% trial budget. — [Xu et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1506.05851)
- **Exploration starvation under throttling/bid cuts. [PROD]** Smart pacing keeps a small "trial rate" on the next layer so it has data when it must speed up. Alibaba baselines "lose valuable opportunities by cutting bids too aggressively" (value ratio 0.36–0.71 vs 0.93 for multivariable PID, offline). — [Xu et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1506.05851); [Yang et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.10928)
- **Overdelivery from delayed charges, with a feedback-loop trap. [PROD]** Delayed attribution charges pushed enterprise overspend to about 11.5% and nearly 25% in two weeks of 2024. Lowering spend made campaigns look less overspent, which moved their finish earlier and repeated the cycle. Fixed by allowing only later start times and recomputing on a slow schedule. — [DoorDash (arXiv 2509.07929)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.07929)
- **Misspecified targets. [GUIDE]** A PID tracks only the given target and "cannot by itself correct a misspecified target". Re-forecast remaining supply periodically, or use a robust online allocation rule. — [Chen, Practical Guide §3.2.3](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.06942)
- **Multiplicative-update (MU) dynamics are dominated. [SIM]** Multiplicative ±ε updates were "dominated by Algorithm 1 on both liquid welfare and regret rate" in the Bing multi-agent simulation. — [Gaitonde et al. §6](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.08674)

### Inferences
- The live bot's pattern, heavy early buying at high prices and then starvation, is the canonical "early depletion / late blackout" failure. A dual-pacing multiplier with a reasonable step size provably bounds it (Lemma 2.2). The equivalent PID-on-cumulative-spend has the same corrective sign.
- The main new risk at 68 rounds is lumpiness: each win costs ~6–10× the per-round target. Updating mu on raw per-round spend (Balseiro–Gur form) would make mu jump after every win and decay during losing streaks, a limit cycle like Snap's BHC failure. The cumulative, normalized, deadbanded, step-clamped error in the Q5 pseudocode is the cited remedy set.
- Cold start is cheap to solve here. Historical rival clearing prices (7.8–9.6) and our own value estimates give mu_init directly, as replay-based initialization in the Practical Guide recommends.
- Mid-game income is like delayed spend in reverse: a cash jump makes the bot look "under plan". If income enters the target fully and immediately, mu can drop sharply and cause a buying burst. Partial crediting (KAPPA < 1) plus step clamping prevents this, following OACP's conservative-pricing rationale.

### Gaps
- No primary source gives recommended numeric PID or dual gains for very short horizons (tens of rounds); all production gains are tuned privately.
- The arXiv 1808.03147 and 2603.07721 failure-mode claims come from search snippets; their full texts were not read.
- I found no study of pacing failure modes in games where a bidder's own wins generate its future budget.
