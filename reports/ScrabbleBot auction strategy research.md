# Bid on Fit, Hold for Seven, Spend Out

SoapConsumers loses because it spends its money on the wrong tiles and then turns its tiles into the wrong words. Its early bids average about 13 on nearly every tile, whatever that tile does for the rack. When its balance drops to 18 it cashes out short words. It finishes with about 12 tiles and 0.49 points per coin spent. LUCENT and Bron finish with 20-22 tiles, hold racks of 8-9 tiles, and make about 70% of their plays 7+ letter words, which pay three times face value. The best-supported strategy is to price each tile by how much it raises the rack's chance of a 7-letter word and how much reward it adds now. That means bidding near zero on filler tiles. It means bidding about the table's median "price to beat" (roughly 12-14 at our tables) on high-lift letters (S, R, N, T, L, E, D, C) while the rack has fewer than 9 tiles. And it means bidding well above market on any tile that completes a 7+ letter word right away. The bot should then play each 7+ word immediately and switch to short-word liquidation only in the last 16 auctions. On reconciling the notes: the live data back the value-maximizer view from sequential-auction theory over ad-style v/(1+μ) shading. Leftover money is worth nothing, and a replayed spending reserve cost 2-5 points. The earlier advice to buy "about 20 tiles at 10 or less each" is wrong on price and right on volume. Replayed against the real top bids, a flat cap of 10 wins only 15 tiles and 63 points, and match winners actually pay more per tile than losers. Finally, the simulator makes us look about 1.5 places better than we really place, partly because it puts six active bots at every table. Fix its table mix and leader models before tuning anything, then confirm the final candidates with about 100-130 live matches per decision.

## Winners turn coins into 22 tiles and 7-letter words, not into cheap prices

The match archive has 455 standard 6-seat matches (24,417 auctions, with both the winning bid and the Vickrey price). Its central fact is an accounting identity: for every seat, **final score = total spent + ending balance − 100** ([a3.py](/Users/pomoika/Documents/GitHub_repo/scrabble/research_notes/ScrabbleBot auction strategy research/scripts/a3.py)). Money has no value at the end of the game. Rewards come back as cash, so the game is about how fast money cycles: each coin buys tiles, the tiles become words, and the words return roughly 0.5-0.7 points, which can be spent again. Compared with the average of the other seats in the same match, winners buy **22.4 tiles vs 9.1**, spend 216.7 vs 101.4, and take **84% of their score from 7+ letter words vs 46%**. Their rack holds 8.0 tiles when they play, against 6.8 for the others ([a2.py](/Users/pomoika/Documents/GitHub_repo/scrabble/research_notes/ScrabbleBot auction strategy research/scripts/a2.py)). Among active seats, **tile count alone explains 78% of the within-match variance in score**. Once tile count and timing are controlled for, average price paid has no independent effect on the odds of winning (odds ratio 1.09, 95% CI 0.93-1.28) ([a2help.py](/Users/pomoika/Documents/GitHub_repo/scrabble/research_notes/ScrabbleBot auction strategy research/scripts/a2help.py)). Win rate climbs steeply with volume: 0% at 5 or fewer tiles, 16.9% at 11-15, 45.1% at 21-25, and 80.4% above 30 ([a2.py](/Users/pomoika/Documents/GitHub_repo/scrabble/research_notes/ScrabbleBot auction strategy research/scripts/a2.py)).

The table shows two connected failures. On the buying side, our own bid trace (1,179 auctions in 19 matches) shows the pattern. Early bids average **13.2**, which is too high for filler tiles but below the 15-16 that rival top bids hold all game. Late in the game, bids fall to 5.7 as the balance drops from 79 to 18 and the rack shrinks to about one tile. We lost most auctions by small margins: in losses, the rival top bid exceeded ours by a median of 6. Bidding just 2 more would have won 125 more auctions ([a6.py](/Users/pomoika/Documents/GitHub_repo/scrabble/research_notes/ScrabbleBot auction strategy research/scripts/a6.py)). The code explains the flat bidding. The investment floor is `max(market price + 2, 6 + 3 × face value)`, scaled only by duplicates and vowel balance. A 55% capital reserve shrinks over time, and any rack with a balance of 18 or less is cashed out ([strategy.ts](/Users/pomoika/Documents/GitHub_repo/scrabble/src/strategy.ts)). On the word side, 46% of our plays are 4 letters or shorter. Leaders play short words only 18-19% of the time, and mostly in the last 16 auctions ([a4.py](/Users/pomoika/Documents/GitHub_repo/scrabble/research_notes/ScrabbleBot auction strategy research/scripts/a4.py)). Short words return little cash, so the money cycle stalls. Starting from the identity above (our inference), score scales roughly with p/(1−p), where p is points per coin. Raising p from our 0.49 to the winners' 0.65 nearly doubles that multiplier, from 0.96 to 1.86.

## A flat cap of 8-10 scores 42-63 points, so the "20 tiles at 10 or less" target must go

The earlier advice to aim for about 20 tiles at 10 or less each compared caps against the *clearing* price. In a Vickrey auction you win only by beating the *top* rival bid. That number is much higher, and the archive records it for every auction. At SoapConsumers tables, a bid beats the top rival bid with probability **7.7% at 8, 16.4% at 10, 31.8% at 12, 50.2% at 14 and 63.3% at 16**. The median price to beat is 12 for vowels and for L, N, R, S, T, 14 for D and G, 17 for B, C, M, P, 20 for F, H, V, W, Y, and 31 for J, Q, X, Z ([a7.py](/Users/pomoika/Documents/GitHub_repo/scrabble/research_notes/ScrabbleBot auction strategy research/scripts/a7.py)). A counterfactual "phantom seventh seat" was replayed through 446 matches with correct accounting: to win, the phantom must beat the recorded top bid, and it then pays that top bid. The table below shows what each flat cap would have achieved ([cf.py](/Users/pomoika/Documents/GitHub_repo/scrabble/research_notes/ScrabbleBot auction strategy research/scripts/cf.py)).

The naive line shows where the error came from: pricing against the clearing price overstates results by 40-110%. **The correction:** keep a volume target of 20-25 tiles, but drop the per-tile price ceiling of 10. Two facts support this. Match winners pay *more* per tile on average than the other seats (10.79 vs 9.97) ([a2.py](/Users/pomoika/Documents/GitHub_repo/scrabble/research_notes/ScrabbleBot auction strategy research/scripts/a2.py)). And even the best rule above plateaus near 80 points, roughly our current level. No uniform cap reaches the winners' level of about 150, because a uniform cap buys whatever is cheap rather than what completes words. A non-selective rule earns about 4.2 points per tile. Winners earn about 8, counting starting tiles.

The price to beat also depends on how many seats are actively bidding, more than on which letter is up. Each extra active bot adds **1.68 (±0.12) to the mean clearing price** and lowers the winner's score by **34.7 (±3.9)**. At tables with 2-4 active bidders, the median price to beat for a vowel is 9. At 5-6 active bidders it is 13 ([a6.py](/Users/pomoika/Documents/GitHub_repo/scrabble/research_notes/ScrabbleBot auction strategy research/scripts/a6.py), [a7.py](/Users/pomoika/Documents/GitHub_repo/scrabble/research_notes/ScrabbleBot auction strategy research/scripts/a7.py)). The data also show a regime shift. After 31 October, 48.1% of seats were inactive and mean prices fell from about 13.3 to 8-9. Over the same period, the score effect of one extra standard deviation of tiles bought rose from +22.8 to +65.3 points ([a2.py](/Users/pomoika/Documents/GitHub_repo/scrabble/research_notes/ScrabbleBot auction strategy research/scripts/a2.py)). So any price threshold must move with the live count of active bidders, which the bot can observe through auction winners and opponents' balances.

## Money is an input, not score, so the value-maximizer view beats ad-style shading

The notes disagree on how much to shade bids and whether to pace spending. The ad-auction literature recommends adaptive pacing: bid `v/(1+μ)` and raise μ whenever spending runs ahead of a budget schedule `B/T` ([Gaitonde et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.08674)). That formula is the optimum for a *utility maximizer*, a bidder for whom every coin spent is a coin of value lost. The autobidding survey shows that for a *value maximizer*, where payment enters only through constraints, the bid multiplier becomes `1/α` and can exceed 1 ([Aggarwal et al.](https://sigecom.org/exchanges/volume_22/1/AGGARWAL.pdf)). Budgeted repeated-auction models reach the same structure from first principles: the right bid is the largest price at which winning-and-paying leaves the value function no worse than losing ([Cai et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1701.02490); [Gummadi, Key & Proutiere](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2016/02/SSRN-id2066175.pdf)). In the final auction of a budgeted sequential second-price game, the equilibrium bid is min(value, budget) ([Ghosh & Liu](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:gamebe:v:113:y:2019:i:c:p:611-632)).

**The value-maximizer view wins, and the archive is the deciding evidence.** Money has zero terminal value by the accounting identity. Yet the average match still leaves 307.7 coins unspent, about 51 per seat ([a1b.py](/Users/pomoika/Documents/GitHub_repo/scrabble/research_notes/ScrabbleBot auction strategy research/scripts/a1b.py)). LUCENT spends down to a median minimum balance of 9, Bron to 12, and winners hold a minimum of 15.2 against 45.7 for losers ([a4.py](/Users/pomoika/Documents/GitHub_repo/scrabble/research_notes/ScrabbleBot auction strategy research/scripts/a4.py), [a2.py](/Users/pomoika/Documents/GitHub_repo/scrabble/research_notes/ScrabbleBot auction strategy research/scripts/a2.py)). Adding a pacing reserve of 40 × remaining/67 coins to the replay *lowered* scores at every cap tested: cap 10 fell from 63.2 to 61.4, cap 12 from 74.8 to 71.9, and cap 16 from 77.6 to 72.1 ([cf.py](/Users/pomoika/Documents/GitHub_repo/scrabble/research_notes/ScrabbleBot auction strategy research/scripts/cf.py)). There is also no cheap phase worth saving money for. The mean clearing price is flat across the game (slope −0.001 per auction, 95% CI −0.005 to +0.002). Top bids actually *rise* in the last 16 auctions, to 18.8 ([a1.py](/Users/pomoika/Documents/GitHub_repo/scrabble/research_notes/ScrabbleBot auction strategy research/scripts/a1.py)). The ad pacing rule's warning about "early depletion and late blackout" fits our trace, where the balance falls from 79 to 18. But the data point to a different cause: we spend on the wrong tiles and get too little cash back. A time-indexed budget schedule fixes neither. Our 55% `capitalReserve` should go.

The pacing side still has a valid point, but it belongs in valuation, not in a reserve. Early in the game a coin is worth more than its face, because it will cycle through several rounds of tile-to-word-to-cash. The shadow price α_t is therefore above 1 early and falls toward 0 at the end. Gummadi et al. likewise find that shading grows as the balance falls ([Gummadi et al.](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2016/02/SSRN-id2066175.pdf)). The archive agrees. A heavy share of spending in the first quarter of the game hurts: the odds ratio for winning is 0.62 per standard deviation. Buying many tiles in the first half helps: odds ratio 2.39 ([a2help.py](/Users/pomoika/Documents/GitHub_repo/scrabble/research_notes/ScrabbleBot auction strategy research/scripts/a2help.py)). Read together, the lesson is to buy volume early but not at completion prices. A field study of fantasy-basketball auctions records the same human bias our bot shows: bidders overpay for their first two or three wins and are then short of money ([Boudreau & Shunda](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/soceco/v62y2016icp8-22.html)). Kelly-sizing theory gives the intuition: betting twice the growth-optimal fraction drives long-run growth to zero ([MacLean, Thorp & Ziemba](https://www.stat.berkeley.edu/~aldous/157/Papers/Good_Bad_Kelly.pdf)). Neither source fits this game exactly, and both support price discipline on speculative tiles, not hoarding cash.

One more piece of the derivation explains why leaders bid so high on completing tiles. It is our inference, not a sourced result. A word's reward counts both as score and as cash that can be spent again. Let R be the reward of the word a tile completes now, and let V_wait be the score-equivalent value of keeping the stem and completing it later. V_wait is roughly the probability of a later completion times (later reward + α × later net cash). The indifference bid is then **b\* = R + (R − V_wait)/α_t**. When completing tiles are plentiful, V_wait is close to R, and b\* falls to about the expected price of the next completing tile. When completers are scarce or time is short, b\* rises well above R, and as α_t goes to 0 near the end it becomes all-in. This one formula covers both behaviours the notes discuss. It reproduces the option-value logic (do not overpay for a common letter when more copies are coming, as with the roughly 2% per-unit decline in Dutch rose auctions ([van den Berg et al.](https://papers.tinbergen.nl/99074.pdf))). It also reproduces LUCENT's completion bids, whose 90th-percentile winning bids run 18-33 on common and mid-value letters and up to 51 on J, Q, X, Z ([a5.py](/Users/pomoika/Documents/GitHub_repo/scrabble/research_notes/ScrabbleBot auction strategy research/scripts/a5.py)). Some ad-literature engineering still carries over. Warm-start parameters from the logs ([Chen, Practical Guide](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.06942)). Calibrate to the highest *other* bid rather than the transaction price ([Wellman, Sodomka & Greenwald](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1210.4915)). Make the endgame finish smooth rather than an abrupt switch ([DoorDash SFF](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.07929)).

The notes also disagree about which letters are good value. Shapley attribution favours vowels at about 0.52 points per coin. A within-match regression favours high-value letters: 0.70 points per coin for J, Q, X, Z against 0.45 for L, N, R, S, T ([a1.py](/Users/pomoika/Documents/GitHub_repo/scrabble/research_notes/ScrabbleBot auction strategy research/scripts/a1.py), [a1b.py](/Users/pomoika/Documents/GitHub_repo/scrabble/research_notes/ScrabbleBot auction strategy research/scripts/a1b.py)). Neither is a causal per-letter value, because buyers pick letters that fit their racks. The way to reconcile them is to value each letter conditional on the rack. A J is worth its face times 3 only if it completes a word, and otherwise it halves the rack's chance of a 7-letter word. The regression's J, Q, X, Z premium mostly measures leaders who bought those letters *as completing tiles*.

## The chance of a 7-letter word jumps from 7% to 53% between 7 and 9 tiles

The scoring rule makes word length the main lever. In the archive, 7+ letter words are 53% of plays but **77% of all reward**: a 7-letter word averages 40.4 points, against 10.9 for a 4-letter word and 18.7 for a 5-letter word ([a5.py](/Users/pomoika/Documents/GitHub_repo/scrabble/research_notes/ScrabbleBot auction strategy research/scripts/a5.py)). With the ENABLE dictionary and a 98-tile bag without blanks, a random rack contains a 7-letter word **7.6% of the time at 7 tiles, 28% at 8, 53% at 9, 73% at 10 and 94% at 12** ([bingo.py](/Users/pomoika/Documents/GitHub_repo/scrabble/research_notes/ScrabbleBot auction strategy research/scripts/bingo.py)). Each tile added from 7 up to 10 raises that chance by 20-25 points, which is why the leaders hold 8-9 tiles before playing. Letters differ sharply in how much they help. Adding S to a 7-tile rack gives a 41% chance of a 7-letter word. R gives 37%, N, T, L and E about 32-34%, and D and C about 30%, while J, Q, X and Z give only 10-13% ([a5.py](/Users/pomoika/Documents/GitHub_repo/scrabble/research_notes/ScrabbleBot auction strategy research/scripts/a5.py)). The best 6-tile stem, AEINST, is completed by 23 of 26 letters ([bingo.py](/Users/pomoika/Documents/GitHub_repo/scrabble/research_notes/ScrabbleBot auction strategy research/scripts/bingo.py)). A rough check (our inference) shows these lifts set sensible bid levels. Adding S raises the 7-letter chance by about 0.34 over a random 7-tile rack, worth about 10-14 points of expected 7-letter reward. An average letter adds about 0.20, worth about 7, and J adds about 0.03, worth about 1. Those figures sit close to the archive's price to beat for those letter classes.

The replay confirms holding out for long words. Letting the phantom play any 5+ letter word instead of waiting for 7+ cut its score from 74.8 to 55.0 at a cap of 12 ([cf.py](/Users/pomoika/Documents/GitHub_repo/scrabble/research_notes/ScrabbleBot auction strategy research/scripts/cf.py)). The leaders' timing points the same way. LUCENT and Bron play a 7+ word on about 70% of plays, usually within 0.2 seconds of buying the completing tile. They switch to short-word liquidation only in the last 16 auctions, where their mean word length falls from about 7.2 to 5.0-5.4, and those auctions hold 46-52% of their plays against 14% of ours ([a4.py](/Users/pomoika/Documents/GitHub_repo/scrabble/research_notes/ScrabbleBot auction strategy research/scripts/a4.py)). Late tiles have less value. A tile bought in the last 16 auctions earns 3.2 Shapley points, against 5.5 for one bought in the first 17, and 14% of late tiles are never played. No word was played after the final auction in any match, so **a bid on the final tile is pure waste** ([a1.py](/Users/pomoika/Documents/GitHub_repo/scrabble/research_notes/ScrabbleBot auction strategy research/scripts/a1.py)).

Scrabble AI shows how to turn this into a rack evaluator. Maven separates each move into score plus rack value. Its board-position term is "usually very close to 0", so the rack term carries the evaluator ([Sheppard](https://web.cecs.pdx.edu/~bart/cs510games-summer2002/papers/maven.pdf)). Removing the rack term costs about 47 points per game against a score-plus-leave player ([Richards & Amir](https://ijcai.org/proceedings/2007/0239.pdf)). A null leave table wins only 37.5% of games against a self-play table over a million mirrored pairs ([wolges leavegen](https://github.com/andy-k/wolges/blob/main/lab/leavegen.txt)). Quackle's tables show which structural lessons transfer. Duplicates are costly: UU is worth −18.8 and II −11.7. Six-tile leaves score best with about 2 vowels (−4.8, against −20.2 with none). Q without U is a burden at −6.79 ([Quackle superleaves](https://github.com/quackle/quackle/blob/master/data/strategy/default_english/superleaves)). Quackle's magnitudes depend on the board and the code is GPL, so they should not be copied. Wolges's MIT-licensed "census" method suits a boardless game well. For every tile multiset, the best playable reward can be computed exactly offline, and a 50/50 blend of the census and self-play tables beat both of its parents ([wolges leavegen](https://github.com/andy-k/wolges/blob/main/lab/leavegen.txt)). A pure probability heuristic can also be strong. Heuri scores mainly on its chance of a bingo next turn, without simulation, and still won 53.35% against Quackle ([ICGA](https://content.iospress.com/articles/icga-journal/icg220201)). That is good evidence that a bid rule built on P(7-letter word) is not a crude shortcut. A draft-bot rule adds a further structural guide: speculate on flexible pieces first, then commit, with the commitment bonus capped ([Ward et al.](https://export.arxiv.org/pdf/2009.00655)).

## The simulator undercounts leaders because it seats six active bots

The simulated models of the leaders score about 58-69. The real leaders average 126-138, and real winners average about 135-153 depending on the table ([a3.py](/Users/pomoika/Documents/GitHub_repo/scrabble/research_notes/ScrabbleBot auction strategy research/scripts/a3.py), [a6.py](/Users/pomoika/Documents/GitHub_repo/scrabble/research_notes/ScrabbleBot auction strategy research/scripts/a6.py)). Much of that gap is the table mix, not bad bot models. LUCENT averages 155.5 at tables with 3 active bidders but 74.6-83.1 at tables with 5-6. Bron averages 154.0 with 3 active bidders and 78.5-89.1 with 5-6 ([a6.py](/Users/pomoika/Documents/GitHub_repo/scrabble/research_notes/ScrabbleBot auction strategy research/scripts/a6.py)). A simulator with six competent bidders at every table will squeeze any leader model to about 80 even if the model is perfect. The real server averages 4.1-4.5 active seats per table, and after 31 October nearly half the seats were inactive. The remaining shortfall, from about 80 down to 58-69, points to missing behaviour: holding 8-9 tiles, bidding high on completing tiles, and playing within 0.2 seconds of completing a word (our inference). The current promotion gate shows the effect. In simulation the baseline's mean rank is 2.15, against a live archive rank of 3.62, so the simulator makes us look about 1.5 places better than reality ([opponent modeling note, local evolution.jsonl](/Users/pomoika/Documents/GitHub_repo/scrabble/data/learning/evolution.jsonl)). That is the "simulation optimization bias" Muratore et al. warn about: policies tuned on a wrong simulator exploit its errors ([SPOTA](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v87/muratore18a.html)).

Calibration is unusually feasible here. The archive records the winner, the winning bid and the second price for every auction. Athey and Haile prove that the highest bid plus the winner's identity identifies asymmetric bidder distributions in second-price auctions ([Athey & Haile](https://gsb-faculty.stanford.edu/susan-athey/files/2022/04/ident_0901_0.pdf)). That allows a direct likelihood fit of each leader's bid function given its public state, which costs far less than simulation-only methods such as ABC ([Carrella et al.](https://www.jasss.org/23/1/7.html)). Every Trading Agent Competition champion paired a probabilistic price model with explicit optimization against it. TacTex'13's wholesale-bidding MDP scored 389.9 against 182.8 with random wholesale bids ([Urieli & Stone](https://object.cloud.sdsc.edu/v1/AUTH_da4962d3368042ac8337e2dfdd3e7bf3/ml-papers-txt/AAAI/2014/tactex13_a_champion_adaptive_power_trading_agent__0db84573.txt)). RoxyBot won TAC Travel by optimizing over sampled scenarios, beating marginal-value heuristics in 1,000-game experiments ([Greenwald et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1401.3829)). ATTac found that planning against an average price often beat noisy per-sample "clairvoyant" planning ([Stone et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1106.5270.pdf)). So rollouts should use a fast greedy policy, not perfect hindsight. Exploiting the rivals is reasonable at the population level. Pluribus avoids opponent exploitation because it needs too many samples ([Brown & Sandholm](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10119653)). Our rivals, by contrast, are fixed-version bots with roughly 270-350 archived seats each. Their bids are published only after each match, though, so any adaptation within a match must rely on live signals such as auction winners and balances.

What counts as "better" also needs care. Under the Plackett-Luce rating update with equal ratings, the expected rating gain for finishing 1st through 6th is proportional to **0.83, 0.63, 0.38, 0.05, −0.45 and −1.45**. Moving from 6th to 5th is worth five times as much as moving from 2nd to 1st. A boom-bust profile with 30% wins and 40% last places has a negative expected rating gain (−0.10). A steady profile placing mostly 2nd and 3rd has a positive one (+0.14) ([Weng & Lin formula, applied in the opponent modeling note](https://jmlr2020.csail.mit.edu/papers/volume12/weng11a/weng11a.pdf)). Required sample sizes are larger than the current practice. With rank SD of about 1.3-1.5, detecting a 0.3-place improvement needs 117-155 live matches against a fixed baseline, 233-310 per arm for a concurrent A/B test, or an expected 100-132 with a Wald SPRT. The current 20-seed simulation gate has a paired SD of about 0.83 fitness units, so it needs about 63 paired seeds to detect its own claimed 0.26 gain ([Fishtest SPRT practice](https://official-stockfish.github.io/docs/fishtest-wiki/Fishtest-Mathematics.html); [evolution.ts](/Users/pomoika/Documents/GitHub_repo/scrabble/src/evolution.ts)). Effects smaller than 0.2 places cannot realistically be detected live.

## A prioritized build plan

The plan below orders changes by expected impact per unit of effort. Each row marks which parts are directly supported by archive or literature evidence and which are our own inference, to be fitted and tested. Thresholds marked "start" are initial values, not findings.

| # | Component | Concrete rule | Evidence-backed | Inference (tune in calibrated sim) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Word timing | Delete the `cashoutBalance` (18) trigger and the `balance > 30` hold condition. Before the last 16 auctions, play only 7+ letter words, and play them in the same auction they become available. Hold the rack at 8-10 tiles. Allow a shorter play only if the rack reaches about 12 tiles with no stem likely to complete. From 16 auctions left, liquidate with the best word each time; from about 6 left, play the best word of any length. Never finish holding tiles. | Leaders hold 8-9 tiles, make 70% of plays 7+ words, play within 0.2 s, and liquidate only in the last 16 auctions. Target moments for a successful build follow from the leaders' profile: about 20-25 tiles per match, at least 60% of plays 7+ letter words, a rack of 8-9 tiles at play, a first play after about 15 seconds, at least 0.6 points per coin, and an ending balance under 20. These are inferred targets, not guarantees, because the archive is correlational and partly mechanical through the accounting identity. Three items are deliberately left out of the plan. Spite or price-setting bids on tiles we do not want, and tracking rivals' racks for contested letters, are supported only by theory ([Benoît & Krishna](https://econwpa.ub.uni-muenchen.de/econ-wp/game/papers/9805/9805001.pdf)), and an accidental win at an inflated price is costly. Full per-auction rollout evaluation in the style of RoxyBot is a later upgrade, worth trying once the cheap three-tier rule is calibrated.

## Conclusion

The research shifts what the bot's problem is. It is not "spending too much", which is the conclusion an ad-pacing frame suggests. It is spending the same amount on every tile and cashing words too short. The archive shows that money is a pass-through and that winners pay *more* per tile. Price discipline matters only as differentiation: near zero for filler, around the market for high-lift builders, and far above market for tiles that complete a word. Every source that fits this game best reaches the same structure: bid the difference in the value function. That holds for budgeted MDP bidding, TAC champions, Scrabble leave tables, and LUCENT's bid pattern reconstructed from the archive. The game's specific twist is that rewards come back as cash, which pushes completion bids above the word's own reward early and lets them reach the whole balance late.

The biggest open risk is measurement, not strategy. The live tables changed regime on 31 October. Table activity shifts the winner's score by about 35 points per active bot. The simulator currently flatters us by 1.5 places. Until the simulator reproduces leader scores by table activity, any tuned parameter is likely fitting simulator errors. The first engineering deliverable should therefore be the calibration gate, built alongside the word-timing fix, which is the one change supported directly by both the leaders' behaviour and the counterfactual replay.
